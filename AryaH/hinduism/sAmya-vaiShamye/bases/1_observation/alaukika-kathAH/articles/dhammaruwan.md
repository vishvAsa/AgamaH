Sometimes, children seem to remember lives other than the one they are leading. Many people argue this is suggestive of reincarnation or rebirth. I don’t know this literature very well, but there is one case I find especially interesting. When I did some research, I found very few people had written about it. So I’ll summarize it for Substack as my contribution to the ongoing miracles/paranormal discourse. I think it is suggestive, though there are unfortunately a number of unknown details about the case.

### Background Questions

##### What is the Pāḷi canon, and what is Pāḷi?

The Pāḷi canon is the Buddhist textual canon used in Theravāda Buddhism, the form of Buddhism practiced primarily in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. “Pāḷi” is the name given in Theravāda commentorial sources for the language in which their canon was transmitted.

##### What is xenoglossy?

Xenoglossy is the purported paranormal ability to speak, recite, understand, read, or write a language one has not learned through normal means.

##### What does Buddhism say about past life recollection?

Generally, Buddhist scriptural sources treat the recollection of one’s past lives as an ability gained through meditation.[1](#footnote-1) An exception is that spirits, both of heavenly and wretched kinds, are explicitly depicted as sometimes spontaneously recalling at least some of their past lives.[2](#footnote-2) However, there are Buddhist sources, including some considered paracanonical in Theravāda Buddhism, in which human beings spontaneously recall a former life.

###### The Story of Alāta and Rucā

For example, there is the _Great Birth Story of Nārada and Kassapa_ (_Mahānāradakassapajātaka_), a paracanonical narrative preserved only in the Theravāda tradition. It describes the Buddha relating a previous life of his attendant, Ānanda, in which Ānanda was a princess named Rucā.[3](#footnote-3)

Rucā’s father, the king, is convinced by a wandering philosopher of various nihilistic doctrines Buddhists tend to consider problematic, including that there is no relationship between one’s present actions and one’s fate in the afterlife.[4](#footnote-4)

First, the king’s own minister Alāta claims to be able to corroborate this view based on his own recollection of a past life in which he butchered many animals; he argues that since he now is presently a king’s minister born in a wealthy general’s family, there must be no relationship between _karman_, or ethical and unethical action, and how one transmigrates.[5](#footnote-5) This same argument was made recently by parapsychologist Ian Stevenson based on his own research into children seemingly recalling past lives:[6](#footnote-6)

> In the cases that I have investigated, I have found almost no evidence of the effects of moral conduct in one life on the external circumstances of another. When I examine the cases that include the feature of a marked difference in socioeconomic status between the families concerned, I can discern no pattern indicating that the vicious have been demoted and the virtuous promoted.

The Buddhist doctrine of how karma can ripen actually predicts this, since the Buddha explicitly said that actions from one’s immediately preceding life may not bear their results in the present life, but might bear them in a future life.[7](#footnote-7) So what Stevenson observed, and what Alāta is depicted as observing in his own case, don’t disconfirm the Buddhist doctrine.[8](#footnote-8)

A short time after this, Rucā converts her father, the king, to the religious life (and away from his doctrine that there is no ethicized afterlife) by relating memories of _her_ previous births.[9](#footnote-9) Since she remembers more than one, she is able to correct Alāta’s mistake by observing the longer course of how karma ripens over many lives.

None of the individuals in this story are depicted as having developed this recollection through meditative skill. Rucā, moreover, is indirectly implied to be quite young at the time when she relates her previous lives to her father; she is called a maiden, is unmarried, lives in her own palace, and has nurses (_dhātimātara_) to whom she gives orders.

###### The Story of Temiya

In another birth-story, the _Birth Story of Mūgapakkha_ (_Mūgapakkhajātaka_), the Buddha relates one of his own past lives as a bodhisattva. He was born as a prince named Temiya, but that birth followed one in heaven, which in turn followed a long time he spent in hell.[10](#footnote-10) The narrative then relates that at the age of one month old, he spontaneously and prodigously recollected his past births in heaven and hell, and also the life that led to him being born in hell in which he was a king.[11](#footnote-11) Fearing the moral dangers of power, he resolves to not allow his family to raise him up as a prince, and refuses to speak or acknowledge anything done to him.

###### Summing Up

From this we can see that Buddhist scripture, including Theravāda Buddhist scripture, affirms the possibility of spontaneously recollecting past lives as a child (as with Temiya), adolescent (as with Rucā), or possibly even adult human (as may have been intended with Alāta). Among the things that can be recollected are Buddhist texts memorized in past lives (see note 2). The lives recalled need not be the ones whose actions are ripening as one’s present circumstances, so an apparent lack of obvious connections between conduct in a remembered life and one’s present fortune or misfortune is accomodated by the doctrine.

### Dhammaruwan’s Own Account

I will refer to the individual involved by his common name “Dhammaruwan” going forward, though he has since ordained as a monk and often goes by his ordination name, Bhikkhu Samādhikusalo. The following details are drawn primarily from an interview conducted with Dhammaruwan by the respected monk and academic Bhikkhu Anālayo on March 21, 2016. Unfortunately, no transcript of this interview is readily available, so I have referred to Bhikkhu Anālayo’s writing on the interview, for which he himself seemingly referred to his own transcript based on his footnotes.[12](#footnote-12)

##### His Present Life

Dhammaruwan was born on November 18, 1968, in Matale, central Sri Lanka. He reported to Bhikkhu Anālayo that at the age of two, he would spontaneously sit in meditation and then chant in a language his mother did not understand, who would try to shut him up. Later, he and his mother moved to Kandy and stayed with Bertie and Rosa Seneviratne, who would end up raising him. When he was about three years old, while his parents were away on a trip to India he was overheard by someone in the house while chanting the _Discourse Setting the Wheel of Dharma in Motion_ (_Dhammacakkappavattanasutta_); upon his parents’ return, they realized he was chanting in Pāḷi. Bertie, impressed, made recordings of the chants and gave them to interested visitors. Bhikkhu Bodhi, who also knows Dhammaruwan well, [was for some reason under the impression that Dhammaruwan’s birth mother specifically told Bertie and Rosa about the chanting, worried he was possessed](https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/interview-with-bhikkhu-bodhi/64#p-101-skype-interview-with-ven-bhikkhu-bodhi-16th-september-2014-1); this detail is not present in Bhikkhu Anālayo’s report of Dhammaruwan’s recollections.

Dhammaruwan reports that as a child he was shy and afraid of the attention. In the interview, he reports a time when, after his story became a bit well-known, he was brought to a monastery to meet its abbot, and was mobbed a crowd that wanted to see him. As a result of this unwanted attention, apparently fearing that Dhammaruwan would lose the ability to chant Pāḷi, made people who came to visit and hear the chanting promise they would not publicize it. But on Dhammaruwan’s own recollection, he was over his childhood visited by:

> several eminent monks, Presidents R. Premadasa (1923–93) and J. R. Jayewardena (1906–96), the Indian meditation teacher Anagārika Munindra (1915–2003), as well as the American professor Ian Stevenson (1918–2007).

Unfortunately, none of these meetings can be independently corroborated except for that with Anagārika Munindra; Munindra’s student, [Joseph Goldstein, in the praise for Bhikkhu Anālayo’s study, writes](https://wisdomexperience.org/product/rebirth-early-buddhism-and-current-research/):

> I first met Dhammaruwan when he was seven years old, when my teacher, Anagarika Munindraji, and I visited him and his family in Sri Lanka.

Dhammaruwan in turn provided many anecdotes for Munindra’s biography.[13](#footnote-13) Dhammaruwan also was acquainted with Bhikkhu Bodhi even as a child, [though Bhikkhu Bodhi claims to have never heard him chant](https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/interview-with-bhikkhu-bodhi/64#p-101-skype-interview-with-ven-bhikkhu-bodhi-16th-september-2014-1), since he could not do it at will, saying:

> He couldn’t do this normally. You know, you’d ask him, “Ruwan recite such and such a text,” and he was not able to do it. But just occasionally he would say to his adopted father, Bertie: “Uncle, uncle, I’m starting to remember. I’m starting to remember!” Then Bertie would get the tape recorder and set it up and record his recitation.

We therefore cannot secure witnesses to Dhammaruwan’s actually chanting Pāḷi Buddhist scriptures from memory as a young boy. What we do have are the recordings of the chanting. But first, what was Dhammaruwan’s own explanation for this apparent xenoglossy?

##### His Recalled Past Life

Dhammaruwan claims to have recalled a life in which he was a Buddhist monk, and student of the eminent 5th century Buddhist commentator Buddhaghosa. In that life, he traveled with Buddhaghosa from India to Sri Lanka (and indeed, Buddhaghosa is recorded to have come to Sri Lanka from India) and settled there. He was a reciter-monk (_bhāṇaka_) and as such had significant portions of the Pāḷi canon memorized, and it was these texts he was chanting from memory. He reports that as a boy he was brought on a tour of the archeological site of Buddhaghosa’s monastery, Mahāvihāra by President J. R. Jayewardena, and claims to have recalled details of the monastery, including where he had lived.

### Defining ~H

The most natural non-paranormal explanations of this sequence of events all involve fraud and/or confabulation:

The recordings could have been made with recitations from a print edition or manuscript rather than from memory, since we don’t have neutral witness testimony of Dhammaruwan’s chanting.

Even if Dhammaruwan chanted from memory, he could have been made to learn the texts from a print edition or manuscript, possibly by his mother, Bertie, or Rosa.

Alternatively, he could have learned them from hearing others recite them (e.g., on the radio), and confabulated or falsified a story about recollecting a past life.

All of these hypotheses have something in common. They involve Dhammaruwan’s versions of the texts being transmitted to him, whether to his eyes in written form or his memory, from contemporaneous sources. Therefore, if one of these is the explanation for the recordings we have, the texts as chanted in those recordings should match contemporaneously available versions of those texts.

##### The Chanting Style

The recordings are clearly of a young boy chanting the texts in question in Pāḷi, presumably Dhammaruwan. Immediately, it is apparent to anyone who has listened to contemporary Pāḷi chanting that the melody and rhythm of Dhammaruwan’s chanting style is highly dissimilar from contemporary styles. If you want to listen to a contemporary recording of a Sri Lankan chanting the _Dhammacakkappavattana_, for example, see [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L6dxFMpfQPE) or [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OAIFo8ZFWlM). Compare these to the recording of Dhammaruwan. The ambitus and tessitura are not the same, in a way that makes the recitation style significantly more melody-focused to my ear (but see the Appendix). There is audibly less melodic movement _within_ single syllables in Dhammaruwan’s style. The colometry still generally tracks syntactic, metrical, and formulaic boundaries (you’ll have to trust me on that one - I am listening to Dhammaruwan with the _Dhammacakkappavattana_ in front of me right now). However, the formulaic boundaries for caesuras are distinct in Dhammaruwan’s style, while still appearing natural.

Regarding this, Bhikkhu Bodhi says:

> Then his intonation is very different from the contemporary Sri Lankan style of chanting: almost just by listening you can tell it’s coming from another era; it’s not the modern style of Sri Lankan chanting.

##### Verifying the Recordings

That the recordings were in fact made when Dhammaruwan was a child is substantiated by two pieces of evidence. The first is the testimony of Bhante Gunaratana, Dhammaruwan’s preceptor in his recent ordination:

> The hearing of that tape happened when I was living in the Washington Buddhist Vihāra between 1968 and 1988, the first Theravāda Buddhist temple in the United States. One day in the early 1970s, somebody gave me a cassette tape saying, “Please listen to this tape.” I listened to it and found the very sweet voice of a little child chanting in Pāli. His Pāli pronunciation was very clear. His chanting style was unique—I had never heard such a chanting style before. I made copies of the tape and shared it with many people. Everyone who listened to his chanting was deeply touched.

The second is Bhikkhu Anālayo’s information from providing a recording to a professional audio-recording and mastering engineer, Bob Katz, here reproduced in full:

> Prior to the first child utterance, I hear genuine “tape print through” which is very hard to simulate in a digital manner. This kind of print through occurs with cassette tapes which have been stored for a very long time. It would be very costly to simulate print through in a way that would fool an audio expert. For example, the print through on this tape has particular characteristics that sound genuine to me and a “mixed up delay” very much like print through that would be difficult to create convincingly through editing and delay. This is evidence that this is a genuine original cassette recording made in the time period between about 1970–1985 and stored for a very long time…The distinct sound character of the built-in microphone of this type of recorder and the characteristic AGC (automatic gain control) are very hard to simulate. Since only the mike preamp of these machines and not their line input had this type of AGC, it would be very difficult and costly to make a modern digital recording and play it back into a very old cassette machine in order to simulate an old recording, and even then the engineer would have to recreate the print through which would be very difficult. So it’s highly unlikely that this recording has been faked by modern digital methods.

Bhikkhu Anālayo adds:

> The engineer notes that he could not detect any rustling of paper throughout the recording, which suggests that the original performance was made from memory…Having examined a photograph of the label on one of the cassettes, the engineer also confirms that this type of cassette was manufactured by Philips, released in 1978 and only made until 1981. Taken together, the results of his examination seem to offer sufficiently strong confirmation that the recording indeed stems from a copy made of an oral recitation performed at some time in the 1970s (or early 1980s, depending on how much time elapsed between the original recording and the copying).

##### The Texts Themselves

Recordings exist of Dhammaruwan chanting:

Major parts of _Mahānidānasutta_ (DN 15)

A few extracts of _Mahāparinibbānasutta_ (DN 16)

_Mahāsatipaṭṭhānasutta_ DN 22

_Anattalakkhaṇasutta_ SN 22.59

All three _Gilānasutta_ discourses SN 46.14-16

The aforementioned _Dhammacakkappavattanasutta_ SN 56.11, _Girimānandasutta_ AN 10.60, _(Mahā)maṅgalasutta_ Khp 5/Sn 258-69, _Ratanasutta_ Khp 6/Sn 222-38, and _Mettasutta_ Khp 9/Sn 143-52

A few stanzas of _Dhammapada_ Dhp

Several sections of the Paṭṭhāna section of the Theravāda Abhidhamma

This is a substantial amount of difficult text for a small child to be able to recite with overall high quality. Moreover, many of these texts are ones that are almost never chanted liturgically and hence would be highly unlikely for him to have heard (e.g., the Paṭṭhāna sections).

##### Errors

Dhammaruwan does occasionally make mistakes. Some are as follows:

(1) In one of the available recordings of Dhammaruwan’s _Mahāsatipaṭṭhānasutta_ (there are two), the interrogative pronoun _katama_ (“which?”) is declined incorrectly, as _katamo_, its masculine nominative singular form. This is a case of [morphological leveling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphological_leveling). In the other recording it is declined correctly.

(2) Again between those two recordings, at one point in one of them the verb _vuccanti_ (“are called”) is misconjugated as singular (_vuccati_). In the other recording it is conjugated correctly.

(3) In the three _Gilānasutta_ discourses there is a phrase according to which the seven factors of awakening lead to awakening. Hence the phrase should use the plural form of “lead to,” _saṃvattanti_, but in two of the discourses Dhammaruwan uses the singular, “leads to,” _saṃvattati_. In the other he conjugates it correctly. I had access to these recordings, as they’re online, I double-checked - Bhikkhu Anālayo is correct that this error occurs, but I actually hear it not as a misconjugation but a metathesis. In the first _Gilānasutta_, I hear Dhammaruwan say "_saṃvanttati_,” misplacing the nasal that rightly occurs in the plural form at the coda of the second syllable instead of the third.

(4) At one point in the _Mahāparinibbānasutta_, Dhammaruwan misdeclines the noun _ādīnavā_, “disadvantages,” as singular, _ādīnavo_, in accordance with the five previous occurrences of the word prior to that section of the text.

(5) At another point in the _Mahāparinibbānasutta_, Dhammaruwan mispronounces _gimhe_, “in the summer,” as _gimhi_, and then immediately follows it with _ºhe_, thus forming _gimhihe_ (not a real word) as though to immediately rectify the error.

(6) At one point in the _Mahāsatipaṭṭhānasutta_, Dhammaruwan repeats an element from a previous list in subsequent lists where it does not belong. More prominently, in multiple parts of that discourse, Dhammaruwan fails to decline certain lists of adjectives with the correct gender, keeping the adjectives declined in accordance with a _previous_ list’s adjectives instead of with the noun featuring in the list presently being recited. Thus, Dhammaruwan in some recordings displays a tendency to mistakenly copy words or morphemes from previous lists to subsequent ones.

(7) In the _Mahānidānasutta_, Dhammaruwan deletes the phrase _esa samudayo_, “this is the origin,” from the phrase _es’ eva hetu etaṃ nidānaṃ esa samudayo esa paccayo_, “just this is the root, this is the cause, this is the origin, this is the condition.” At another point, a similar deletion of a redundant word occurs. By the way, the reason why these texts have so much redundancy is because they were edited in ancient times for oral transmission, and that recension process predates their being written down.

To me, error (6) is strongly suggestive that Dhammaruwan himself did not know Pāḷi when making these recordings. It is systematic grammatical error that would be unlikely to be made just by a slip of attention for someone with a command of the language. Moreoever, it also suggests that if someone had made Dhammaruwan recite, they also were not acquainted with Pāḷi.

Error (5) is just weird. Not the error - that’s a slip of the tongue - but the correction. It suggests that in that case, he did actually notice the mistake, and knew what the correct pronunciation was.

Overall, though, the recitation proceeds with almost no mistakes, and with everything clearly pronounced. The observable errors are ones consistent with slips of attention or the tongue, except for (6).

### The Philological Evidence

Recall that our alternative hypothesis involves these recordings being performed, either directly or via memorizing, from versions of the relevant texts that would have been available to Dhammaruwan to hear or be shown in Sri Lanka in the 1970s. I’m going to assume it’s pretty unlikely that he somehow was given access to a hiterto unknown manuscript containing distinct recensions of these texts. In fact, a number of his variant pronunciations are distinctly uncharacteristic of the Sri Lankan Pāḷi accent.[14](#footnote-14) So the plausible alternative hypothesis should trace the origins of his versions to the available print editions.

All of these texts were available in print at the time in four full editions of the Pāḷi canon: the Sri Lankan, Burmese, Thai, and Pali Text Society (PTS) editions. Moreover, the Sri Lankan recitation liturgical collection known as the _Catubhāṇavārapāḷi_, editions of which would have been available, contains the three _Gilānasutta_ discourses, the _Girimānandasutta_, the _(Mahā)maṅgalasutta_, the _Ratanasutta_, and the _Mettasutta_ (corresponding to roughly 25% of the total length of chanted text). So Dhammaruwan could have produced his chants from these sources.

##### Minor Variants

On many occasions, Dhammaruwan’s chants differ from these sources in minor ways. I’m not going to go through all of them. Bhikkhu Anālayo has some nice tables [in the book](https://www.buddhismuskunde.uni-hamburg.de/pdf/5-personen/analayo/rebirthearlybuddhism.pdf) if you want to see just how much his chanting varies systematically from extant recensions. Instead, I’m going to focus on the variants that to me suggest most strongly that Dhammaruwan’s recordings do not correspond to the contemporaneous sources.

On all occasions where Dhammaruwan pronounces the term _seyyathīdaṃ_, “for example,” a term repeated fourteen times in four different texts, he splits it into _seyyathi idaṃ_. This is not found in any of the contemporaneous sources, even as a variant in the footnotes. If one were to separate the term, the more natural separation would be into _seyyathā_ and _idaṃ_, since that’s what the compound is actually formed using. _No_ extant recension has this variant.

Dhammaruwan quite distinctly pronounces the word for “disappearance” in its form _atthagama_, as only the PTS edition does, where all the Asian editions use the variant _atthaṅgama_. But in a total of 22 other places, including 9 that involve a systematic variant pronunciation of the same term, Dhammaruwan diverges from the PTS edition in ways sometimes corresponding to one of the Asian editions - but not always the same one - and sometimes not corresponding to any.

##### Major Variants

Again, there are many occasions where Dhammaruwan’s chants differ from these sources in a major way. But the following are the weirdest.

###### Anatomical Parts

Twice in the _Mahāsatipaṭṭhānasutta_ and once in the _Girimānandasutta_, there is a listing of the anatomical parts of the body as meditation objects. In none of the editions of the Pāḷi canon does the _Girimānandasutta_ include the brain as part of the list; the Burmese and PTS editions of the _Mahāsatipaṭṭhānasutta_ include it, but only in the footnotes as a variant, and not in the body of the text.

But Dhammaruwan always includes the brain (_matthaluṅga_) in the list. Even the version of the _Girimānandasutta_ in the _Catubhāṇavārapāḷi_ does not mention the brain. Moreover, while _another_ text in the _Catubhāṇavārapāḷi_ that lists the anatomical parts includes the brain, it places it at the end of the list. But Dhammaruwan places it in the middle of the list, after feces.

Other Theravāda texts aside from the ones Dhammaruwan chanted, like the _Khuddakapāṭha_, _Paṭisambhidāmagga_, and _Visuddhimagga_ (Buddhaghosa’s most famous composition) do include the brain as part of the list, sometimes putting it at the end, and sometimes after feces. So Dhammaruwan’s placement of the brain in the list follows Theravāda precedent. But it is not a precedent actually visible in any sources for the texts Dhammaruwan chanted that would have been available to him.

The only known version of the _Girimānandasutta_ that includes the brain is a 14th century version of the _Girimānandasutta_ that is only extant in a Tibetan translation from that time, but of course, this could not have been available to him (in this life).

This suggests that either Dhammaruwan had been exposed to a different text featuring the list from the ones he actually chanted, extant editions of which do contain the brain, and then he memorized the list from those sources but applied it to the _Mahāsatipaṭṭhānasutta_ and _Girimānandasutta_, or that his recensions of those texts is simply not based on any extant versions.

###### The Acclaim of the Gods

At the end of the _Dhammacakkappavattanasutta_, after the Buddha has given his first sermon, the text describes the rejoicing of the orders of gods. In Buddhist cosmology, the orders of gods can be split into three groups, the gods of the _kāmadhātu_, _rūpadhātu_, and _arūpadhātu_, or Sensuous, Form, and Formless Realms. The gods of the Formless Realms are held to abide continously in meditative absorptions that prevent them from interacting with anything, and as such are never mentioned rejoicing or participating in the Buddha’s dispensation. In all extant editions of the Pāḷi canon, the orders of gods who rejoice in the Buddha’s first sermon in the _Dhammacakkappavattanasutta_ proceeds by listing the different orders of Sensuous gods by name, but then the text does not list the gods of the Form Realm. Instead, it refers to them collectively as _brahmakāyikā devā_, “gods of the world of Brahmā.”

However, when Dhammaruwan chants the end of the _Dhammacakkappavattanasutta_, he does not give this single reference to the _brahmakāyikā devā_. Instead, he lists successively the orders of Form Realm gods, thus adding a significant chunk of relevant text:

> brahmapārisajjā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…brahmapārisajjānaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā brahmapurohitā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…brahmapurohitānaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā mahābrahmā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…mahābrahmānaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā parittābhā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…parittābhānaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā appamāṇābhā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…appamāṇābhānaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā ābhassarā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…ābhassarāṇaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā parittasubhā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…parittasubhānaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā appamāṇasubhā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…appamāṇasubhānaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā subhakiṇhakā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…subhakiṇhakānaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā vehapphalā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…vehapphalānaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā avihā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…avihānaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā atappā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…atappānaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā sudassā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…sudassānaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā sudassī devā saddamanussāvesuṃ…sudassīnaṃ devānaṃ saddaṃ sutvā akaniṭṭhakā devā saddamanussāvesuṃ

The only extant version of the _Dhammacakkappavattanasutta_ to contain this addition is that found in the _Catubhāṇavārapāḷi_. But recall that of the total chanted text, only 25% is found in the _Catubhāṇavārapāḷi_ in the first place; it does not include the three DN sources or the _Anattalakkhaṇasutta_, for instance. And the texts that could have been based on the _Catubhāṇavārapāḷi_ in many ways vary from it as well, even though in this specific way Dhammaruwan’s _Dhammacakkappavattana_ follows that source.

Specifically, if we consider the thirteen variants (one of which involves three instances) in Dhammaruwan’s chanting overall that do not correspond to _any_ of the editions of the Pāḷi canon, only three turn out to correspond to the _Catubhāṇavārapāḷi_. The remainder differ not only from all editions of the canon, but also the _Catubhāṇavārapāḷi_. For example, there is the aforementioned unique inclusion of the brain in the _Girimānandasutta._

Considered with the fact that around 75% of the material Dhammaruwan chants is not found in the _Catubhāṇavārapāḷi_ anyway, it seems unlikely that Dhammaruwan’s _Dhammacakkappavattana_ displays this addition of the orders of Form Realm gods because his chants are based on the _Catubhāṇavārapāḷi_.

### Summary

What we have are a series of recordings most probably made during a time when Dhammaruwan was in fact a child on cassete tapes, in which we can hear a young boy, presumably Dhammaruwan, chanting Pāḷi Buddhist scriptures with very few errors of recitation, but a large number of systematic minor pronunciation variants that differ from any extant recensions of those scriptures from which he might have been taught to recite them. Moreover, his recitations display major variants - additions and deletions - including some that follow predictable patterns of textual accretion, such as the addition of the brain to the list of anatomical parts or the expansion of the abbreviated orders of Form Realm gods. In the case of the _Mahāsatipaṭṭhānasutta_, the addition of the brain corresponds only to the footnotes of the Burmese and PTS editions. In the case of the _Girimānandasutta_, the addition of the brain corresponds to no extant versions, including that of the _Catubhāṇavārapāḷi_; this is the case despite the _Catubhāṇavārapāḷi_ being the only place where the _Dhammacakkappavattana_ expands the orders of gods as Dhammaruwan does.

Dhammaruwan is now a Buddhist monk, and as such would likely commit a serious confession-demanding offense if he maintained his story of past-life recollection while knowing that it was a lie. He is also described well by all who know him and speak of him, and has been involved in no scandals that might impugn his character. But he _does_ continue to maintain this story.

Note how this case provides material and philological evidence not available in cases like those studied by Ian Stevenson and Jim Tucker, which have been criticized by philosophers like Evan Thompson (and thanks to [Amod Sandhya Lele](https://open.substack.com/users/3962137-amod-sandhya-lele?utm_source=mentions) for citing this excerpt in his [post](https://loveofallwisdom.substack.com/p/you-still-have-to-naturalize-karma) so that I would see it):

> Although Stevenson’s presentation of these cases often makes for compelling reading, all the evidence is anecdotal and derived from interviews where there is a large amount of room for false memory and after-the-fact reconstruction. The interviews weren’t conducted directly with the children when they first reported the memory, but only some time later, so there had been plenty of time for the child to assimilate information gotten from family members and to repeat it as if it were his or her experience. And sometimes the children weren’t interviewed at all; only family members were. Finally, it’s hard to know how to assess whether a memory report about a past life exceeds chance probability, and critics have pointed to a number of serious flaws in Stevenson’s statistical reasoning. For these reasons, I don’t find Stevenson’s evidence convincing, though it does seem possible in principle to investigate claims of past life memories using scientific methods (p. 290, with endnote references deleted).

In this case, the evidence is not based on interviewing the child or their parents for details about the life remembered, and then trying to find out if there is a person about whose life the child could not have learned through normal means who matches their memories. The evidence is not really based on the details of the past life recollection at all. It is based on an _ability_ to produce alternative recensions of texts corresponding to no contemporaneous versions.

I am not really good at playing the Bayesian game like everyone else, so I’ve presented the case based on the available materials I could find so that others can consider it. I think there are a number of problems with the case, since many details are lacking. The biggest problem is the lack of anyone alive aside from Dhammaruwan who can tell us that they personally saw Dhammaruwan recite one of these texts from memory. But the philological evidence to me seems to partly bypass that worry, since it challenges any theory on which Dhammaruwan was taught or made to recite.

### Appendix - Miraculous Musicology Measurements

I think the distinctive chanting style Dhammaruwan has is exquisite, and fascinating. So I decided to do a serious comparison with contemporary Sri Lankan chanters, as well as _Dhammaruwan himself_ as an adult, now that he’s internalized a more mainstream style. [frank lucid24](https://open.substack.com/users/314390094-frank-lucid24?utm_source=mentions) kindly [uploaded](https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/4bv-sted-metta-chanting-kn-khp-9/8385) a collection of mp3 files of different chanters reciting the _Mettasutta_, including Dhammaruwan as a child and as an adult and a few others. So using those files, I’ve used AI to do a bit of analysis. My tracks for comparison are trimmed versions of Dhammaruwan’s recordings, child and adult, and Bhante Gunaratana’s recording. Thanks to Claude, I didn’t have to actually learn Praat for this, and I can confirm some of what I said about Dhammaruwan’s childhood style. All measurements were made with Praat. I can give the files and full settings and data to anyone who wants them.

First, on the ambitus. Dhammaruwan, as a child, used a melody traversing 17 semitones - nearly 1.5 octaves. Dhammaruwan as an adult and Bhante Gunaratana both stay within about 5 semitones - around a major third. So Dhammaruwan’s childhood chant style ranges more than three times the melodic span of either adult reciter measured.
