RGB regards[^315_314] Gaṇa-pati as the leader of the *gaṇas* of Maruts, the hosts of Rudra. He connects Gaṇapati's elephant-head with Rudra's association with forests and wild places as also with Śiva's apparel of the hide of elephant.[^315_315] RGB further [[P316]] thinks that Gaṇa-pati became the lord of wisdom and learning[^316_316] because of his identification with Bṛhaspati who is referred to as *gaṇa-pati* in *ṚV* II. 23. 1. All this, however, does not carry conviction. Various theories have been advanced in recent years regarding the origin of Gaṇa-pati. Attention is, for instance, drawn to the mention of *karāṭa* (*puruṣa*), *hastimukha* (*vakra-tuṇḍa*), and *dantin* in the Gāyatrī-mantra occurring in *MS*. II. 9. 1 and *TA* X. 1. 5,[^316_317] which would take the cult of the elephant-headed god back to the fourth-third century B. C. The figure of Gaṇeśa is also often derived from *oṃkāra*. On the other hand, it is suggested that Gaṇa-pati was not a god of Vedic origin but that he was a totem of a Dravidian tribe and functioned as a god of fertility,[^316_318] or that he was originally a Yakṣa, or that he was an ancient elephant-genie of the jungle, who, when seen in his popular aspect, was a libidinous figure later used in the symbolism of Tantrism.[^316_319]

[^315_314]: *Op. cit.*, pp. 210–214.

[^315_315]: HERAS (*TC* 3, pp. 151–213) thinks that the god's elephant head is due to the Nāgas. He further adds that Gaṇa-pati has derived many characteristics from his supposed brother Skanda, who, according to him, is the only real son of Śiva. There can be little doubt about Skanda having originally belonged to the popular non-Vedic religious complex. However, in his *The Cult of Skanda-Kārttikeya in ancient India* (Calcutta, 1970), Asim Kumar CHATTERJEE deals, among other things, with the delineation of Skanda in the Vedic literature. In that connection, he draws attention to the Skanda-gāyatrī in *MS*, the Skanda-yāga as an *AVPar.*, and to several references in *Ch. Up.*, *Pār GS*, and *Baudh DS.* According to Sukumar SEN (*Indo-Iranica* 4, pp. 27 ff.), Kumāra referred to in *ṚV* X. 135 is the prototype of the post-Vedic Skanda (which is not at all likely) and a counterpart of Iranian Śraosha. Also see: V. M. BEDEKAR, "Kārttikeya (Skanda) in Sanskrit literature with special reference to the Mahā-bhārata: from a folk-spirit to the chief war-god," *ABORI* 56, 141–177.

RGB found no mention of Gaṇa-pati in any of the Gupta inscriptions examined by him.[^316_320] However, images of that god ascribable to the sixth century A. D. are to be seen in the Gupta [[P317]] temples at [[Bhurma|Bhumra]] (District Panna, Madhya Pradesh) and Udayagiri hills near Vidiśā, which fact clearly points to the existence of the worship of Gaṇeśa in the Gupta period. This is further confirmed by the fact that Varāhamihira (beginning of the sixth century) has prescribed in his *Bṛhat-saṃhitā* detailed instructions in connection with the fashioning of the images of Gaṇeśa.

[^316_320]: *Op. cit.*, p. 212.

It is worthy of notice that images of Gaṇa-pati are found all over Asia (except West Asia).[^317_321] Indeed, the earliest dated image of Gaṇeśa, known till now, is said to be the one in the rock-cut temple at Kung-hsien in China. It is assigned to 531 A. D. But it is doubtful whether one can speak of the spread in those regions of Asia of any Gaṇeśa-cult as such, for, there have rarely been found any temples exclusively dedicated to that god. In Indonesia, for instance, the images of Gaṇa-pati are to be seen mostly in the sanctuaries for Śiva. It may also be pointed out that the Buddhists adopted Gaṇa-pati in their own pantheon and that he travelled to distant lands with that religion.[^317_322]

[^317_321]: Cf. M. K. DHAVALIKAR, "Gaṇeśa beyond the Indian frontiers," *Vivekananda Comm. Vol.*, pp. 1–14.

[^317_322]: It is pointed out that Gaṇa-pati in South-east Asia is generally a Hindu deity, while, in the Far East, he is a Buddhist one.
