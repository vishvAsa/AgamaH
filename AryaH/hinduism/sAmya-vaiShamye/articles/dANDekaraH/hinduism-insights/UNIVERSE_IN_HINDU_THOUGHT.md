## Lecture One[^149_1]

[^149_1]: Three Special Lectures delivered at the Bangalore University.

It has been said that man looks inward, he looks around, and he looks upward, and out of his response and reaction to this threefold 'looking' of his are born his religion and philosophy. To put the same thing in other words, man (as implied by 'looking inward'), universe (as implied by 'looking around'), and god (as implied by 'looking upward') are the three basic themes of man's religio-philosophical speculations. You are, perhaps, aware that I have already written and published two monographs—one on "Man in Hindu thought"[^149_2] and the other on "God in Hindu Thought".[^149_3] I am now engaged on preparing a monograph on "Universe in Hindu thought", and I propose to present before you some aspects of that subject—indeed, just a few aspects which can be conveniently dealt with in these three lectures—mainly in the light of Vedic evidence. The title of the series of these lectures, accordingly, needs to be changed to "Universe in Vedic Thought."

[^149_3]: Published elsewhere in this Volume.

The subject of the "Universe in Hindu (or rather, Vedic) Thought" can be studied mainly from three points of view—namely (1) of cosmogony-cosmography (that is, of the origin, evolution, and nature of the universe and of the plan and structure of the universe), (2) of mythology (considered mainly under three heads: [a] creation and organization of the universe as a function of the divinities; [b] deification of cosmographical regions like heaven, earth, etc.; and [c] assignment of cosmographical regions as abodes of gods), and (3) of ethics-eschatology. The desire to know the origin and nature of things may be said to be almost inborn in man. Accordingly, [[P150]] attempts are seen to have been made by man, even in the infancy of the human race, to explain the origin and development and the plan and structure of the world which surrounded him and which naturally aroused his interest, in other words, to evolve some kind of cosmogonical myth. Indeed, it is difficult to imagine any period in the history of man when he had not formulated a cosmogonical myth. It should also be remembered that the cosmogonical notions of man have always influenced and are often reflected in his ritual. The parallelism between myth and ritual must never be lost sight of while studying any ancient religion.

To begin with what I have elsewhere characterised as the protohistoric Hinduism,[^150_4] we have to admit that hardly any material has become available which would help us to visualise its cosmology-cosmography. The evidence brought forth by archaeological excavations for the reconstruction of the material civilization of the protohistoric period of India's history is no doubt fairly rich, but attention has also been appropriately drawn to 'the notorious incapacity of material symbols to represent the true contents and affinity of a religion or belief'.[^150_5] Notwithstanding this, some speculations regarding the beliefs of the Indus people about the universe, particularly from the eschatological point of view, have been hazarded on the basis of very meagre and obviously unconvincing evidence. It has, for instance, been suggested that the Indus people believed in a sky-world, or rather in a solar world, to which the spirits of the dead were supposed to repair after death.[^150_6] An ellipsoid urn recovered from the cemetery at Harappa (denoted by H 206 *b*) is presumed to depict a funeral scene. It is claimed that the therianthropic figure (the upper part of the body being that of a peacock and the lower one that of a man) shown standing between the animals represents the subtle body (*sūkṣma śarīra*) of the dead person and the animals his guides to the land of bliss or the solar world. One finds oneself on a surer ground when one comes to the age of the Veda. The Vedic literature is fortunately rich in speculations relating to the various aspects of the subject of the universe. Broadly speaking, prayers and mythological traditions constitute the principal content of the *Ṛg-veda*. But the Ṛgvedic poet-priests have by no means ignored cosmogony, which, as a matter of fact, often represents the first manifestation of religious thought. Their cosmogonical speculations either form part of the general pattern of their mythology or are expressed in the form of independent cosmogonical myths. Naturally enough, we cannot speak of any single uniform cosmogonical theory of the *Ṛg-veda* as such. It would seem that various explanations about the origin and nature of the universe were put forth though none of them had been finally confirmed. Further, it has been rightly suggested that any particular cosmogonical theory has to be related to the man and not to the age. It also needs to be remembered that these various theories are by no means mutually exclusive. One more thing: In my various writings I have consistently advocated the view that the major mythology of the *Ṛg-veda* is essentially evolutionary in character.[^151_10] I must, however, state here that, unlike the Ṛgvedic mythology, the cosmogony [[P152]] of the *Ṛg-veda* can be ill subjected to an evolutionary interpretation.

[^151_10]: Cf. Dandekar, "Some Aspects of Vedic Mythology: Evolutionary Mythology", *University of Ceylon Review*, Vol. 12, 1–23. Also see: Dandekar, *Vedic Mythological Tracts*, Delhi 1979.

A mention may be made at this stage of a few more points, which, as a matter of fact, may be said to apply to Hindu cosmogony as a whole. According to the *Ṛg-veda*, creation is not a single definite act — it is regarded as ever proceeding.[^152_11] It is a process and not a single event. But, unlike the *Purāṇa*-cosmology, the Vedic cosmology does not speak of repeated creations. A Vedic poet asserts that the heaven and the earth have been created but once. *ṚV* VI. 48. 22 tells us: *[[sakṛdd ha|sakṛd dha]] dyaur ajāyata sakṛd bhūmir ajāyata*. In the Ṛgvedic passage, *sūryā-candramasau dhātā yathā-pūrvam akalpayat*, etc. (X. 190. 3), the word *yathā-pūrvam* has to be understood in the sense, “according to the proper order.”[^152_12] This is confirmed by the reference in the *Vājasaneyi-Saṃhitā* (40. 8), *yāthātathyato 'rthān vy adadhāt*. Secondly, the concept of creation *ex nihilo* is practically unknown to Hindu thought. As a matter of fact, such a concept is generally not to be found in any of the ancient cosmogonies. Creation usually implies the transformation of the potential into the actual or the establishment of cosmos in the place of chaos. It is, indeed, in this latter sense that the Ṛta is often characterised as the source of the phenomena: *dyāvā ha kṣāmā prathame ṛtenā 'bhiśrāve bhavataḥ* (*ṚV* X. 12. 1). In the only Ṛgvedic passage, which seems to suggest that something was produced out of nothing, namely, *devānāṃ pūrvye yuge 'sataḥ sad ajāyata* (X. 72. 2), the word *asat* actually means ‘primordial non-differentiation or distinctionlessness’ rather than ‘nothing’.[^152_13] And, thirdly, Vedic cosmogony does not have any teleological significance.

[[P153]] The creator, whenever he is mentioned, is hardly ever represented as having been moved by any idea of executing any deliberate plan. It is perhaps on account of this that the creator as such does not play any prominent role in Hindu religious thought which rarely concerns itself with the design, object, or purpose of the world.

It is well known that the creation of the universe is ascribed in the *Ṛg-veda* to various divinities. The relevant Ṛgvedic passages are too many to be mentioned here. The creation of the universe, indeed, constitutes almost an inevitable feature of the full-fledged mythological personalities of the Vedic gods. The cosmological activity of the Vedic gods is often described as a work of art and is commonly expressed through the metaphors of ‘building’, ‘joining’, ‘weaving’, ‘moulding’, etc.[^153_14] Such references, however, have, in many cases, merely a rhetorical rather than cosmogonical import. It may be emphasized, at this stage, that, broadly speaking, in Vedic mythology, the cosmogonical themes and the legends of gods do not show any organic unity. There do, however, exist, in Ṛgvedic mythology, some divinities which are more or less exclusively connected with the creative activity. Among these a special reference deserves to be made to Tvaṣṭṛ and Viśvakarman. Tvaṣṭṛ was originally regarded as having been responsible for the creation of the universe; but when, in the evolutionary mythology of the *Ṛg-veda*, history was mythologised and the human hero Indra was transformed into a cosmic god, he usurped Tvaṣṭṛ's function as the creator in the same way as he usurped Trita Āptya's function as the rain-god.[^153_15] It has also been suggested that Tvaṣṭṛ must have belonged to extra-Vedic tradition, that the Ṛbhus were represented in the *Ṛg-veda* as his friendly rivals, and that, after he had been divested of his cosmological functions, he became a fertility-god and a god of handicraft.[^153_16] As for Viśvakarman, his personality [[P154]] seems to have resulted from a conscious effort to represent a unity of godhead through the creatorship of the universe.[^154_17]

A tendency has also become evident in recent years to see in the Indra-Vṛtra-fight an original Aryan myth of creation.[^154_18] It is pointed out that Indra slays with his *vajra* the power of resistance of the inert chaos (*vṛtra*), splits the primordial mountain on which Vṛtra was residing, and thereby brings out the goods of life symbolised by fire (Agni-Sūrya) and water (Āpaḥ-Soma). By slaying Vṛtra, Indra also separates the heaven from the earth, which event represents an early stage in the process of creation. This view is slightly modified by KUIPER[^154_19] who has suggested that the Indra-Vṛtra-fight reflected only the second stage in the process of creation. Waters represented the beginning of the universe. Within those waters, there emerged the primordial mountain. Indra directed his assaults against this mountain which had been Vṛtra's abode, slew Vṛtra, and thereby advanced the cosmogonical process. KUIPER wonders whether all this cannot be construed as just a macrocosmic projection of a necessarily individual recollection of the embryogonic experience. Indra's great creation-act, he further asserts,[^154_20] is repeated every morning and ‘leads up every year, at the time of the winter solstice, to a new crisis.’[^154_21] It would, however, seem that the cosmogonical traits in the Indra-mythology were not original but that they were superimposed on it at a later stage in the evolution of Indra's personality and character as a part of the mythologisation of history. Indra is obviously a newcomer in Vedic [[P155]] mythology — he is *ānujāvara*[^155_22] — and there must have been in vogue some cosmogonical myth even before Indra came to be glorified as the supreme god of the *Ṛg-veda*. As has been already indicated, Indra must have superseded his 'father' or 'grandfather' Tvaṣṭṛ and must have thereby taken over to himself the cosmogonical functions of the latter.

As against Indra's, Yama's role in the cosmological act must be regarded as more basic and essential. There occurs in the *Ṛg-veda* a rather obscure — but, for our present purpose, a highly significant — reference to Yama. *ṚV* X. 13. 4 reads:

> *devebhyaḥ kam avṛṇīta mṛtyuṃ prajāyai kam amṛtaṃ nā 'vṛṇīta |*
> *bṛhaspatiṃ yajñam akṛṇvata ṛṣiṃ priyāṃ yamas tanvaṃ prā 'rirecīt ||*

"For the sake of gods, indeed, did he choose death; for the sake of procreation, verily, did he abjure immortality. They (that is, the gods) made Bṛhaspati the *ṛṣi*, (himself) the sacrificial offering. (Thus) Yama (willingly) surrendered his own dear body."

There is ample evidence in the *Ṛg-veda* to show that Yama was a god: *ṚV* I. 83. 5, for instance, speaks of *yamasya jātam amṛtam*. As such he was entitled to immortality (*amṛtatva*). Notwithstanding this, Yama, we are told, opted for death — he surrendered his own dear body in the sacrifice of the gods for the sake of procreation. Yama was a god-man — an immortal who willingly suffered death. This is evidently a description of the primeval sacrifice performed by the gods for the purpose of the creation of the universe and the procreation of the human race. In that sacrifice, Yama was the principal priest and also the principal sacrificial offering. It was a cosmic sacrifice which was [[P156]] characterised by the self-immolation of a god-man and which was intended for *prajā* (procreation).[^156_23]

[^156_23]: See: Dandekar, "Yama in the Veda", *Vedic Mythological Tracts*, 118–140.

An extended version of this cosmogonical speculation occurs in the famous *Puruṣa-Sūkta* (*ṚV* X. 90). The Puruṣa, whose characterisation as *puruṣa* suggests his humanness but whose epithets, *amṛtatvasya īśāna*, etc., clearly indicate his divineness, was, like Yama, a god-man. In the primeval sacrifice of the gods, the principal oblation was this Puruṣa himself (*puruṣeṇa haviṣā devā yajñam atanvata*). The various parts of the universe (as also of the human society) issued out from the various limbs of the sacrificed primal person.

Attention may be drawn to another significant motif associated with the self-immolation of the god-man, Puruṣa or Yama. This god-man was also a hermaphrodite — a being constituted simultaneously of male and female. This is suggested by the Puruṣa-Virāṭ concept in the *Puruṣa-Sūkta* and the Yama-Yamī concept in the Yama-mythology. We, thus, have in the *Ṛg-veda* the cosmogonical theory, according to which the universe is believed to have been the result of the self-immolation of a hermaphrodite god-man. It may also be pointed out that the idea of sexual congress of a male and a female as the starting point of the creation of the universe is implied in the relationship between Puruṣa and Virāṭ (X. 90. 5), Aditi and Dakṣa (X. 72. 4), Yama and Yamī (X. 10), and even Dyauḥ and Pṛthivī (VI. 70. 3).

The Hindus have conceived of the universe as being infinite in time and space. Though this notion becomes particularly emphatic only in the post-Vedic literature, we do get some intimations of it even in the *Ṛg-veda*. A reference may be made in this connection to the mythological concept of Aditi who personifies 'infinity'[^156_24] or 'eternity'[^156_25] or 'immensity'[^156_26] or [[P157]] 'unbondage.'[^157_27] She represents a sort of a common womb or substratum of all existence — she is all that has been born and all that will be born: *aditir jātam aditir janitvam* (*ṚV* I. 89. 10). She is, indeed, all in all — the one in whose lap is the wide atmosphere (*yasyā upastha urv antarikṣam*, *AV* VII. 6. 4) and after whom are born all the gods (*tāṃ devā anu ajāyanta*, *ṚV* X. 72. 5). But, in spite of its infiniteness,[^157_28] the universe is not a chaos. It is well-supported,[^157_29] well-ordered, and well-regulated — in other words, it is a cosmos.[^157_30] This is perhaps one of the most significant features of Vedic cosmogony.

The ancestors of the Vedic Indians lived in close proximity to nature. It was but to be expected that, in that stage of their cultural life, they should have been deeply impressed by the vastness, brilliance, compassion, and bounty of nature. It was, indeed, this primary impression of theirs which the Vedic poets sought to project symbolically in their religious ideology through the mythological concept of the cosmic parents represented by the dual divinity, Dyāvā-pṛthivī. From the point of view of comparative mythology, however, it would appear that the personalities of the Father Sky (*dyauḥ pitā*) and the Mother Earth (*pṛthvī mātā*) in the Vedic mythology, unlike those of their counterparts in the other Indo-European mythologies, remained more or less arrested in their growth. The main reason for this, I believe, was that, in their mythology, the Vedic Aryans did not put so much emphasis on the fact that the nature around them was vast, brilliant, and bountiful as on their uncanny experience that, in spite of its vastness, nature was extremely well-ordered and well-regulated. It was out of this profound realisation that they soon evolved the concepts of the cosmic law, Ṛta, and its administrator, Asura Varuṇa.[^157_31] Eventually, Ṛta became the pivot of their cosmogonical speculations.

[^157_31]: See: Dandekar, "Asura Varuṇa", *Vedic Mythological Tracts*, 28–67.

[[P158]] Varuṇa enforced his cosmic law,[^158_32] Ṛta, in a very subtle way, by binding down the various aspects of creation by means of 'ropeless bonds' as it were (*setṛbhir arajjubhiḥ*, *ṚV* VII. 84. 2). The very word *varuṇa* is derived from the root *vṛ* "to bind." Elsewhere the Ṛgvedic poets speak of Varuṇa holding up fast the crown of the tree (presumably, the heaven) in the 'foundationless' space (*abudhne rājā varuṇo vanasyo 'rdhvaṃ stūpaṃ dadate pūta-dakṣaḥ*, *ṚV* I. 24. 7)[^158_33] and of the great master fitting in together the two wide, deep, and well-founded worlds in the 'beamless' region (*urvī gabhīre rajasī sumeke avaṃśe dhīraḥ śacyā samairat*, *ṚV* IV. 56. 3). And Varuṇa could accomplish such mysterious feats because he was Asura. One of the most fundamental beliefs of the Vedic Aryans was that, though the universe was varied and multiform in outward appearance, it was unitary and uniform in essence. A single all-pervading and more or less somatic magic potence permeated through the various constituents of the universe and thereby invested them, so to say, with existence and life. Indeed, such a belief was common to many ancient ideologies. The Vedic Aryans seem to have denoted this cosmic magical potence by the term *asu*. God, man, beast, tree, etc., might appear to be different from one another for all practical purposes, but, as possessors of *asu*, they were all essentially identical. The differences among them were quantitative rather than qualitative. The word *asura* is derived from *asu* (the magic potence) and *ra* (a possessive termination): Asura was the possessor of the greatest quantity of *asu*. Varuṇa was Asura *par excellence* in the Vedic mythology, and so he could effectively administer the cosmic law, Ṛta. The epithet *asura* is, no doubt, applied to some other Vedic gods, but that is only to suggest that, compared to the other constituents of the universe, such as man, beast, etc., the gods possessed a greater amount of [[P159]] *asu*. Verily, it is in view of this peculiar *asu*-ideology of the Vedic Aryans that I have ventured to characterise the religion of the early *Ṛg-veda* as Pan-asuism rather than Pantheism.[^159_34]

The assumption of the creation of the universe out of some primal matter is common to many ancient cosmogonies, and the element which is often thought of in this connection is waters. The primal character of waters is taken for granted, and no [[P160]] question is generally raised in respect of the origin of waters themselves. Even Viśvakarman is said to have been preceded by this primordial substance or substratum.[^160_38] Waters were "the primitive medium in the womb of which is fashioned everything that exists":[^160_39] *tam id garbhaṃ prathamaṃ dadhra āpo yatra devāḥ sam agacchanta viśve* (*ṚV* X. 82. 6), and the poet of *ṚV* X. 129. 1 asks about them: "What was that deep bottomless water?": *ambhaḥ kim āsīd gahanaṃ gabhīram*.[^160_40] Verily, in all periods of the history of Hindu thought, waters have been regarded, in different ways, as the material source of the universe. According to the *Hiraṇya-garbha-sūkta* (*ṚV* X. 121), the primal spirit or the highest god, who is later identified with Prajā-pati, originated in the primeval waters as Hiraṇya-garbha, the Golden Embryo, which, incidentally, is sometimes described as the principle of fire floating upon waters, and subsequently started the cosmogonical process. The Ṛgvedic Hiraṇya-garbha may be identified with *hiraṇya-vetasa* – the golden reed – referred to in *Atharvaveda* X. 7. 41. Hiraṇya-garbha may also be related to the myth of Aditi (who can be equated with primeval waters) and Mārtāṇḍa.[^160_41] Then there is also the cosmogonical theory (*ṚV* X. 129) that, in the primordial condition which could not be described either as *sat* because it was uncharacterisable or as *asat* because it possessed the potentiality of evolving, there arose, at the outset, *kāma* or the original impulse for creation, and this marked the beginning of the subsequent evolution of being. Further, in this very context may be mentioned the creative role of *vāk*.[^160_42] As for the cosmogony of the *Atharvaveda*, by and large it speaks of the Prajā-pati-like highest god, conceived under different names and forms such as *rohita* (XIII. 3–4), *anaḍvān* (IV. 11), *kāla* (XIX. 53–54), *kāma* (XIX. 52), as the creator of the universe.

[[P161]] Speculations more or less similar to the Vedic ones can be traced in various ancient cosmogonies. For instance, waters are regarded as the primal matter in the Babylonian, Egyptian, and Greek cosmogonies. The Egyptian and Greek cosmogonies also have the notion of the cosmic egg. Vedic Kāma may be said to correspond with Greek Eros, and the sexual relationship between the sky and the earth is referred to in Hebrew cosmogony. It may also be pointed out that, unlike Vedic cosmogony, the ancient Iranian cosmogony is one of opposition. The entire cosmic process, according to the latter, consists of a series of beneficent creations by Ahura Mazdah and maleficent counter-[[creatious|creations]] by Ahriman.

Very little has been said in the *Ṛg-veda* about the actual process and order of the creation of the universe. A reference may, however, be made in this connection to a couple of passages. In one rather obscure passage (X. 72. 3–4) we are told: in the first epoch of the gods, *sat* was born from *asat*,[^161_43] after it (*sat*) originated the world-regions (*āśāḥ*), and that one subsequent to the couchant (*uttāna-pad*: what this *uttāna-pad* denotes is not at all clear);[^161_44] the earth was born from the couchant and from the earth originated the world-regions. Elsewhere (X. 190. 1–2) it is said: Ṛta and *satya* originated from *tapas*, from it (it is not clear what this 'it' denotes; or does *tataḥ* mean 'thereafter'?) the night, and therefrom the agitating ocean; from the agitating ocean was born the year (perhaps, symbolising time), which, ruling over all that winks, organizes days and nights. I think that the abstruse Ṛgvedic stanza (X. 27. 23), *devānāṃ māne prathamā atiṣṭham*, etc., also refers to the process and order of the creation of the universe.

## Lecture Two

An essential feature of Vedic—indeed, of the entire Hindu—cosmography is the system of *lokas*[^162_1] or worlds or cosmographical regions. There is, however, to be seen considerable vagueness, inconsistency, and confusion about the various details regarding these *lokas*. For instance, the conflicting Vedic statements in respect of the number of *lokas* constituting the universe cannot be reduced to any kind of uniformity. Usually the Vedic poets speak of a dual division of the universe. The universe is said to be constituted of *dyaus* (heaven) and *pṛthivī* (earth). Incidentally, the word *dyaus* is translated both as 'sky' and 'heaven'. However, in the Vedic contexts, I prefer the rendering 'heaven.' The word *svarga*, which is invariably employed in later literature to denote heaven, occurs but once in the *Ṛg-veda* (X. [[75|95]]. 18). The Vedic poets express the idea of the whole universe through the mention of the heaven and the earth together—however, not uncommonly in contradictory terms, such as that the heaven and the earth are the parents of gods[^162_2] and that they are generated by gods.[^162_3]

[^162_1]: In his *Loka: World and Heaven in the Veda*, Amsterdam 1966, Gonda has discussed in great detail the concept of *loka*, particularly as it occurs in the Veda.

[^162_2]: Cf. *ṚV* I. 159. 1; VII. 53. 1.

It was believed that the heaven and the earth were originally joined together. This is faintly suggested in several Ṛgvedic passages,[^162_4] but is more clearly stated in the *Brāhmaṇas*. For instance, the *Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa* (IV. 27) says: *imau vai lokau saha āstām*.[^162_5] They were subsequently separated and held apart. One of the features of the enforcement of the cosmic law by Varuṇa [[P163]] was that he propped asunder the two worlds, namely, the heaven and the earth, wide as they were: *vi yas tastambha rodasī cid urvī* (*ṚV* VII. 86. 1). It is, again, presumably with reference to the phenomenon of the separation from each other of the heaven and the earth that Indra is said to have made all things whirl round: *yene'mā viśvā cyavanā kṛtāni* (*ṚV* II. 12. 4). But Indra also stabilized the earth which was trembling as the result of the separation: *yaḥ pṛthivīṃ vyathamānām adṛṃhat* (*ṚV* II. 12. 2) and supported the heaven: *yo dyām astabhnād* (*ṚV* II. 12. 2). It is suggested that, as the second stage in the cosmogonic process, Indra, by killing Vṛtra, brought about this cosmic dualism of the upper world and the nether world. It may, however, be incidentally mentioned that the explanation of the term *ānujāvara* on the assumption of the older gods of the nether world, like Varuṇa, etc., and the newer gods of the upper world, like Indra, etc., is by no means convincing.[^163_6] Nor can one accept the view that the notion of the separation of the sky and the earth is a cosmic reflection of the dual organization of the social group.[^163_7] The separation of the heaven and the earth from each other resulted in the emergence of *antarikṣa* or the midregion. Indra is said to have measured out the midregion wider: *yo antarikṣaṃ vi mame varīyaḥ* (*ṚV* II. 12. 2). It is, indeed, suggested that coming into being at creation consisted largely in being able to pass between the heaven and the earth.

The references in the *Ṛg-veda* to the three *lokas* (worlds or regions), namely, *dyaus* (heaven), *antarikṣa* (midregion), and *pṛthivī* (earth),[^163_8] are so frequent that one is inclined to think that the Vedic Aryans rather assumed a tripartite division of the universe. The heaven is then referred to as *tṛtīya*,[^163_9] *uttama*,[^163_10] or [[P164]] *parama*,[^164_11] while the earth is *upara*[^164_12] or *kṣam*.[^164_13] Sometimes these three worlds are mentioned and then one of the three is additionally mentioned in the same passage.[^164_14] There are also indications that three of one of these three worlds, for instance, three *dyāvaḥ* or three *pṛthivyaḥ*, are made to denote the whole universe. It would seem that Hindu cosmographical speculations were greatly influenced by the numbers 'three' and 'seven'—by the number 'three' generally in the Vedic texts and by the number 'seven' in the *Purāṇas*. The *Vājasaneyi-Saṃhitā* (8. 60) mentions the usual three *lokas*, namely, *dyaus*, *antarikṣa*, and *pṛthivī*, and further adds *yaṃ kaṃ ca lokam*, thus suggesting the possibility of the existence of more than those three *lokas*. *Nāka* or the firmament or the heavenly vault[^164_15] is sometimes mentioned besides the usual triad as a region higher than that triad.[^164_16] The purpose of *nāka* seems to have been to hold the stars or to serve as the basis of the heavenly ocean which encompasses the whole world. A passage in the *Atharvaveda* (IV. 14. 3) seems to speak of a realm of celestial light (*svar jyotiḥ*) as a region further beyond *nāka* (*divo nākasya pṛṣṭhāt svar jyotir agām aham*). However, the *turīyaṃ dhāma* referred to in *ṚV* IX. 96. 19 does not seem to possess any cosmographical significance, though Sāyaṇa, and following him LUDWIG and GELDNER, understand the phrase as denoting the moon.

The expression *ṣaḷ urvīḥ*, six wide ones, occurs in the Ṛg-veda three times,[^165_21] but in none of these passages does its exact significance become clear. It may be presumed that *ṣaḷ urvīḥ* more or less correspond with the *ṣaḍ rajāṃsi* of ṚV I. 164. 6 and represent the six wide regions which make up the universe, for, it is asserted that no world exists outside of the *ṣaḷ urvīḥ*: *na yābhyo bhuvanaṃ kaccanā 're* (ṚV VI. 47. 3). They may thus be understood to denote the three heavens and the three earths referred to above. But Sāyaṇa, commenting on ṚV X. 128. 5, says that the six *urvī*s are, according to the scripture (presumably, the *Āśvalāyana-Śrauta-sūtra* [[1.|I.]] 2. 3 and *Śāṅkhāyana-Śrauta-sūtra* I. 6. 4), *dyaus*, *pṛthivī*, day, night, waters, and plants. However, the *Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa*, which is the oldest text explaining the term *ṣaḍ urvīḥ*, speaks (I. 5. 1. 22) of *agniḥ*, *pṛthivī*, *āpaḥ*, *vājaḥ*, *ahaḥ*, and *rātriḥ*, while elsewhere *dyaus*, *pṛthivī*, *āpaḥ*, *oṣadhayaḥ*, *ūrk*, and *sūnṛtā* are also mentioned in this connection. Obviously, all this is quite formulaic. In a passage in the *Atharvaveda* (VIII. 9. 16), six *dyāvāpṛthivī*s and six *urvī*s are mentioned side by side perhaps in syntactic parallelism.

It is needless to point out that the various words used in the Ṛg-veda in connection with the universe and its different regions throw much helpful light on the Vedic notions about the nature and structure of the universe. The significance of many of these words—such as *pṛthivī* (extended), *mahī* (great), *urvī* (wide), and *bhūmi* (*ī*)-*bhūman* (that which has come into being; secondarily, place of becoming or prospering; amplitude) (all these denoting the earth); *dyaus* (shining), *rocana* (realm of light), and *vyoman* (from the root *yu* with *vi*: that which has been separated and held apart; some scholars derive the word from the [[P166]] root *ve* 'to weave', *vyoman* then meaning 'web of light') (these usually denoting the heaven); and *antarikṣa* (from *antari* and the root *kṣī*: that which lies in the middle; the word *antarikṣa* is also analysed as *antar* and the root *īkṣ* and then explained as transparent; a third meaning which has been suggested is: 'shining in the middle')[^166_22]—is self-evident. The concept of *loka* in the Veda has been studied in great detail by Professor GONDA,[^166_23] and it has been shown, among other things, that *loka*, originally connected with light, represents, more particularly, 'a place or position of religious or psychological interest', or 'a place which has come into contact with "power"', or 'room to exist in freely and without hindrance and obstruction.' It has been further stated that the term *loka* denotes 'the diversification of the primordial cosmic unity.' Similarly it has been shown that, in the ultimate analysis, the term *[[bhavana|bhuvana]]* may be understood in the sense of 'the whole aggregate of what has come and is coming into being.'[^166_24] In the Ṛg-veda, the word *rajas*, when used in singular, denotes the midregion; when used in plural, it denotes the worlds or regions in general. That word is derived from the Indo-European *rēg* 'to stretch out,' and originally means 'that which has been stretched out' 'space', and more specifically the space above the earth. Heinrich ZIMMER assumes[^166_25] the existence of a *rajas* beneath the earth to account for the sun's course from the west towards the east during the night. In this connection, he refers to the Ṛgvedic passages, such as, VI. 9. 1, VII. 80. 1, and IX. 68. 3. But this is rather far-fetched. Most of the Ṛgvedic passages, in which the word *rajas* occurs, clearly point to the region or regions above the earth. But the word seems to have eventually acquired a special sense. The portion of the upper space attached to the earth was darker than the one which was attached to the heaven. The former came to be known as *rajas* and the latter as *rocana*. The phrase *[[kṛṣṇa|kṛṣṇaṃ]] rajas* is not [[P167]] uncommon in the Ṛg-veda.[^167_26] The possibility is also suggested of the dual *rajasī* being an abbreviated form of *rajas* plus *rocana*. It must, however, be remembered that, in the Ṛg-veda, the word *rajas* is not everywhere used in the special sense mentioned above. Incidentally, it may be pointed out that the word *rajas*, hardly occurs in the Ṛg-veda in the sense of 'darkness' or 'dust' as such.[^167_27]

It has been already pointed out that the Vedic poets often think of Dyāvā-pṛthivī as a single mythological entity. Dyāvā-pṛthivī (also referred to as *dyāvā-kṣāmā*, *dyāvā-bhūmī*, or *rodasī*) play not an unimportant role in Vedic mythology. Six whole hymns in the Ṛg-veda are addressed to this dual divinity, and it is also mentioned in over 100 isolated passages. Dyāvā-pṛthivī are celebrated as the primeval parents (*pūrvaje pitarā*, ṚV VII. 53. 2; X. 65. 8)—as the cosmic father and mother (*pitā mātā ca bhuvanāni rakṣataḥ*, ṚV I. 160. 2; or *dyaur me pitā janitā nābhir atra bandhur me mātā pṛthivī mahī 'yam*, ṚV I. 164. 33).[^168_32] But, as has been already pointed out, unlike in many ancient mythologies, the character of Father Sky and Mother Earth as the basic cultic [[P169]] figures was not sufficiently developed in Vedic mythology.[^169_33] Dyāvā-pṛthivī are said to have given birth to the gods—they are *devaputre* par excellence[^169_34]—though they are also sometimes said to have been generated by the gods. However, in the Ṛg-veda, there is hardly any direct reference to the marriage between Dyaus and Pṛthivī, such as the one which we meet with, perhaps for the first time, in the *Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa* IV. 27: *tau saṃyantāv etaṃ deva-vivāhaṃ vy avahetām*. The latter are together called *pitarā* or *mātarā* or *svasārā*. However, their mythological growth was manifestly arrested even in the Ṛg-veda, and they are more or less completely subordinated in Vedic ritual.

There is only one short hymn (consisting of only three stanzas) in the *Ṛg-veda* (V. 84) which celebrates Pṛthivī. In the *Atharvaveda* also there is only one hymn to Pṛthivī (XII. 1), but it is very much longer (it consists of sixty-three stanzas) and far more significant than the Ṛgvedic hymn. What strikes one most about the Atharvavedic Pṛthivī-Sūkta is that it does not contain mere conventional mytho-poetic cliches or any remote mystic allusions. Through it, the poet seeks to make us feel the direct impact of this concrete tangible earth, on which we live, as acutely as he himself has felt it. He speaks, in a purely pragmatic manner, of the vital relationship of the visible earth with man. The hymn describes in detail and in a transparent language the physical features of the earth, its extraordinary bounty, its moral-ethical foundations, its mythological-ritual connections, and, above all, its character as the great integrator of the human community. Both these hymns clearly show that, in the Veda, Pṛthivī is more important as locality than as a divinity. But the earth is not merely a conglomeration of rocks, stones, trees, etc. It is not only a physical entity, it also has a distinct moral-ethical character. In the famous hymn to Pṛthivī (*AV* XII. 1), the Atharvavedic poet speaks of truth, Ṛta, penance, sacrifice, etc., being the true foundation of the earth (st. 1). He, however, waxes really eloquent while dilating upon the bounty and liberality of Pṛthivī. The earth is rich in food,[^175_83] cows, horses, and other treasures (4, 44: these and similar other numbers occurring hereafter denote the numbers of stanzas in *AV* XII. 1) (*nidhiṃ bibhratī bahudhā guhā vasu maṇiṃ hiraṇyaṃ pṛthivī dadātu me*, *AV* XII. 1. 44)—the [[brokenup|broken-up]] earth is said to give out treasures (*kṣāś cit tatṛdānā*, *RV* IV. 28. 5)—and adequately sustains and strengthens whatever breathes and moves (3), all bipeds and [[P176]] quadrupeds (15). The earth is the mother, the rain the father, and the man their child (*mātā bhūmiḥ putro 'haṃ pṛthivyāḥ, parjanyaḥ pitā sa u naḥ pipartu*, *AV* XII. 1. 12). "What I dig out of thee, may that grow again quickly" (*yat te bhūme vi khanāmi kṣipraṃ tad api rohatu*, *AV* XII. 1. 35)—this is the Atharvavedic poet's prayer to the earth. The earth is, indeed, *viśvaṃ-bharā* par excellence (6).

The Atharvavedic poet further goes on to say that it was on the earth that the gods overcame the Asuras and man's ancestors unfolded themselves (*yasyāṃ pūrve pūrva-janā vi cakrire, yasyāṃ devā asurān abhy avartayan*, *AV* XII. 1. 5). Aśvinau measured the earth, Viṣṇu bestrode it, and Indra, vanquishing the Dasyus, lorded over it (10, 37). The earth is also said to make the sacrificial ritual possible (*yasyāṃ sado havir-dhāne yūpo yasyāṃ ni mīyate*, *AV* XII. 1. 38).

Finally, the earth is rightly glorified as the essential basis of the life of the human community. It is the great gathering place affording full scope to the vigorous activity (18) of man and his various organizations such as *grāma*, *araṇya*, *sabhā*, *saṃgrāma*, and *samiti* (56). What is, however, particularly remarkable is that the earth is thoroughly impartial and equally supports all kinds of men—foolish as well as weighty or respectable, good as well as evil (*malvaṃ bibhratī guru-bhṛd bhadra-pāpasya nidhanaṃ titikṣuḥ*, *AV* XII. 1. 48). It also integrates people practising different religious rites and speaking different languages (*janaṃ bibhratī bahudhā vivācasaṃ nānā-dharmāṇam*, *AV* XII. 1. 45). A Ṛgvedic passage (I. 72. 9) seems to imply that the earth severed its connections with the gods in order to be able to be more closely associated with men.

The third of the normal Vedic world-triad, namely, the midregion, does not figure in the *Ṛg-veda* with any prominence. The two words which commonly denote the midregion, namely, *antarikṣa* and *rajas* (in singular), have been already discussed. It has also been pointed out that no clear evidence is available in the *Ṛg-veda* to support the assumption of *rajas* beneath the earth [[P177]] to account for the sun's course during the night.[^177_84] The statement that, in the primordial condition, there was no *rajas* (*nā 'sīd rajaḥ*, *RV* X. 129. 1) presumably refers to the situation when the heaven and the earth were still joined together. That the poet should add, in the same passage, that, at that time, there was also no *vyoman* (= that which has been separated or held apart, that is, the heaven)[^177_85] would thus seem significant. For, Indra is credited with having measured out *antarikṣa* by separating *pṛthivī* and *dyaus*.[^177_86] The entire process of the origination of *pṛthivī*, *rajas*, and *dyaus* out of the primeval ocean is indicated in *RV* X. 149. 2: *ato bhūr ata ā utthitaṃ rajo 'to dyāvā-pṛthivī aprathetām*. It may be noted that the context makes it necessary to understand *dyaus* for *dyāvā-pṛthivī* in this passage. In view of the cosmographical position of *antarikṣa*, it is understandable that the gods are described as moving through the midregion.[^177_87] In another significant passage (*RV* X. 149. 1), Savitṛ, who actually represents but an aspect of Asura Varuṇa,[^177_88] is said to have calmed down the faltering earth by means of rivets, fastened the heaven in the supportless space, and milked the *antarikṣa* which neighed like a horse: *savitā yantraiḥ pṛthivīm aramṇād askambhane savitā dyām adṛṃhat | aśvam ivā 'dhukṣad dhunim antarikṣam atūrte baddhaṃ savitā samudram*. This last activity evidently refers to the phenomenon of rain falling from the midregion overspread with thundering clouds. It is, however, not unlikely, that it refers to the gushing forth of the primeval waters. One may also note that, in this passage, *antarikṣa* is compared to a sea sustained in the immeasurable (*atūrte*) space. It is suggested that, in the last *pāda* of this stanza, the adjective *atūrte* is to be connected with the noun *rajasi* which is to be supplied. In that case, here, *rajas* will have to be regarded as a wider concept than *antarikṣa*. In [[P178]] another passage, *antarikṣa* is, on the one hand, differentiated from *pṛthivī*, while, on the other, it is brought into specially close contact with *dyaus*: *pṛthivī naḥ pārthivāt pātv aṃhaso 'ntarikṣaṃ divyāt pātv asmān* (*RV* VII. 104. 23).

It is only rarely that *antarikṣa* is referred to, even by implication, as a divinity as such. In *RV* III. 54, like Pṛthivī, Antarikṣa also is included among the Viśve Devāḥ (st. 19). But there is an interesting, though rather obscure, hymn in the *Atharvaveda* (I. 32) which seems to glorify *antarikṣa* in a special way. The theme of that hymn may as well be said to be the "apotheosis of *antarikṣa*." The poet begins by proclaiming that a great mystery is going to be pronounced (*mahad brahma vadiṣyate*: I read *vadiṣyate* for *vadiṣyati* occurring in the Śaunaka text), namely, that 'what makes the plants breathe is not on the earth nor in the heaven': *na tat pṛthivyāṃ no divi yena prāṇanti vīrudhaḥ*; it is thereby implied that it is in the *antarikṣa* (1). The midregion (I read *antarikṣam* for *antarikṣe*) is the station of those (life-giving potencies) — it is the foundation of all being (*asya bhūtasya āsthānam*) (2). When the two trembling worlds — namely, the heaven and the earth, while being separated from each other — fashioned out the third world, namely, the *antarikṣa*, the latter was all watery even as the streams of the sea (3): *yad rodasī rejamāne bhūmiṃ* (I read *bhūmiṃ* for *bhūmiḥ*) *ca nir atakṣatam, ārdraṃ tad adya sarvadā samudrasye 'va srotyāḥ*. It may be recalled that the midregion is often regarded as a celestial ocean. This pervading space, that is, *antarikṣa*, has, as it were, penetrated into the one (that is, *dyaus*) — it is supported upon the other (that is, *pṛthivī*) (4): *viśvam anyām abhī 'vā 'ra tad anyasyām adhiśritam*. The significance of this hymn from the point of view of cosmogony-cosmography needs to be adequately appreciated.

## Lecture Three

Today, I should like to begin with a brief excursus on the cosmological-cosmographical speculations of the *Brāhmaṇa*-texts. It is said that, when speculation is once set going in the *Brāhmaṇas*, the result of its trend is to lead its followers in an intellectual chaos. This is, no doubt, a rather harsh indictment, but it cannot be said to be altogether unwarranted. We have seen that, broadly speaking, three main trends of cosmogonical thought are evident in the early Veda, namely, (1) beginning with a mythological figure, (2) beginning with some material principle, and (3) beginning with some abstract principle. All these three trends are reflected in the *Brāhmaṇas*, but with this noteworthy difference: (*a*) There is a greater emphasis on a single Personal Creator or a more patently anthropomorphic All-God, namely, Prajā-pati, as being the principal character in the cosmogonic drama; (*b*) the other two trends, namely, of a material principle and an abstract principle being the starting points of the cosmogonical process are organically mingled with the basic Prajā-pati-legend; and (*c*) all these trends are eventually given a distinct ritualistic orientation. It must, however, be conceded that the cosmogonical legends in the *Brāhmaṇas*, by and large, tend to confirm the dictum that mythology is a kind of primitive metaphysics.

Though, as has been pointed out, there is, in the *Brāhmaṇas*, an unambiguous stress on what may be called the personalistic theories of creation, there is still no suggestion of the creator having any motive or design for creation. He is hardly ever represented as executing any deliberate plan. It often seems as if the creator is moved primarily by a desire to allow an outlet to the exuberance of his energy—by an impulse to multiply himself. The whole universe thus becomes just a manifestation of some [[P180]] effusive force. Incidentally, it is this attitude which later developed into the doctrine that the universe represents but the *līlā-kaivalyam*.

The most typical cosmogonical myth of the *Brāhmaṇas* has Prajā-pati as its central figure: *so 'yaṃ puruṣaḥ prajā-patir akāmayata | bhūyānt syāṃ prajāyeye 'ti | so 'śrāmyata sa tapo 'tapyata | saḥ śrāntas tepāno brahmai 'va prathamam asṛjata trayīm eva vidyām | sai 'vā 'smai pratiṣṭhā 'bhavat ... tasyāṃ pratiṣṭhāyāṃ pratiṣṭhito 'tapyata | so 'po 'sṛjata vāca eva ... | so 'kāmayata | ābhyo 'dbhyo 'dhi prajāyeye 'ti | so 'nayā trayyā vidyayā sahā 'paḥ prāviśat | tata āṇḍaṃ sam avartata | tad abhy amṛśad astv ity astu bhūyo 'stv ity eva tad abravīt ... atha yo garbho 'ntarā 'sīt so [['grir|'gnir]] asṛjata ...* (*Śata-patha-Br.* VI. 1. 1. 8 ff.) "Now this Person Prajā-pati desired, 'May I be more (than one), may I be reproduced.' He toiled, he practised austerity. Being worn out with toil and austerity, he created first of all the Brahman, the triple science. It became to him a foundation.... Resting on that foundation, he (again) practised austerity. He created the waters out of Vāk ... He desired, 'May I be reproduced from these waters.' He entered the waters with that triple science. Thence an egg arose. He touched it. 'Let it exist; let it exist and multiply', so he said ... Now the embryo which was inside was created as [[agri|Agni]]...."[^180_1] And so the process continues with Agni, Pṛthvī, etc.

[^180_1]: This and subsequent translations of the *[[Śatapath-Br.|Śatapatha-Br.]]* passages are reproduced, with slight changes in some places, from EGGELING's translation of that *Brāhmaṇa* (Sacred Books of the East Series).

There are several variations of this basic theme, some features of the other cosmogonical trends, mentioned above, being integrated with it. Thus, for instance, according to *ŚPBr.* XI. 5. 8, Prajā-pati, who was alone in the beginning, desired, 'May I exist, may I be generated', practised penance, and eventually created the three worlds, namely, the earth, the *antarikṣa*, and the heaven. He heated these three worlds, and from them, thus heated, respectively three lights were produced—Agni, Vāyu, and Sūrya.

[[P181]] From these lights were produced the three Vedas, and from these latter, *bhūḥ*, *bhuvaḥ*, and *svaḥ*. And this legend is then set into the framework of the discussion about a sacrificial procedure. *Ait. Br.* V. 32 has a more or less similar legend: *prajā-patir akāmayata prajāyeya bhūyān syām iti | sa tapo 'tapyata | sa tapas taptve 'mā̐l lokān asṛjata pṛthivīm antarikṣaṃ divam | tā̐l lokān abhy atapat | tebhyo 'bhitaptebhyas trīṇi jyotīṃṣy ajāyanta ...* etc. All this occurs in the context of the Agnihotra and the duties of the Brahman priest. In another version occurring in *ŚPBr.* VIII. 4. 3, Prajā-pati is said to have sought the help of the Prāṇas in having his desire to procreate fulfilled. The *Aitareya-Āraṇyaka* II. 1. 8 speaks of the Puruṣa creating, by his speech, etc., the earth with fire, etc.

Sometimes, waters are represented as having preceded Prajā-pati (*ŚPBr.* XI. 1. 6. 1 ff.): *āpo ha vā idam agre salilam evā 'sa | tā akāmayanta kathaṃ nu prajāyemahī 'ti | tā aśrāmyaṃs tās tapo 'tapyanta | tāsu tapas tapyamānāsu hiraṇyam āṇḍaṃ sam babhūva.* In a year's time, a man, this Prajā-pati, was produced therefrom. He broke open this golden egg. There was then, indeed, no resting place; only this golden egg, bearing him, floated about for as long as the space of a year. At the end of a year he tried to speak. He said *bhūḥ*—this became the earth; he said *bhuvaḥ*—this became *antarikṣa*; he said *svaḥ*—this became the heaven. We are then further told: *sa (= prajā-patiḥ) ātmany eva prajātim adhatta | sa āsyenai 'va devān asṛjata ... atha yo 'yam avāṅ prāṇaḥ | tenā 'surān asṛjata.* The *Taittirīya-Saṃhitā* also has the legend of the waters and the cosmic egg.

Besides a material principle, which is almost invariably the waters, abstract principles also are mentioned as the starting points of the creation-process. In *ŚPBr.* VI. 1. 1. 1, *asat* is said to have been there in the beginning: *asad vā idam agre āsīt.* The question is then asked: What does this *asat* imply? The answer given is: *ṛṣayo vāva te 'gre asad [[āsit|āsīt]].* The Ṛṣis, verily—it is they that the *asat* was in the beginning. And the Ṛṣis are then said to be identical with the Prāṇas; *ta (= prāṇā) iddhāḥ sapta nānā puruṣān asṛjanta.* But they soon realised that they would not be [[P182]] able to generate with those seven separate persons. They, therefore, thought, and *etān sapta puruṣān ekaṃ puruṣaṃ akurvan.* The one Person so formed out of the seven persons was Prajā-pati. And, thereafter, it was Prajā-pati who took the lead in creating the universe. According to the *Taittirīya-Brāhmaṇa* (II. 2. 9), the successive elements in the cosmogonic process are: *asat*, *kāma* or desire, *tapas*, and creation in connection with which the urinary bladder of Prajā-pati is said to have been the origin of the ocean. Another passage from the *ŚPBr.* (X. 5. 3) takes its cue from the famous Nāsadīya-Sūkta. It says: *ne 'va vā idam agre 'sad āsīn ne 'va sad āsīt | āsīd iva vā idam agre ne 'vā 'sīt | tadd ha tan mana evā 'sa.* This mind wished to become manifest, to become more defined (*niruktataram*), more substantial (*mūrtataram*). We also come across (*ŚPBr.* XI. 2. 3) the *brahman*, which, as the first principle, created the gods. And, having created the gods, it made them ascend these worlds—Agni this world, Vāyu the *antarikṣa*, and Sūrya the heaven. The further part of this passage is particularly interesting from the point of view of cosmography or the structure of the universe. For, it goes on to say: And the deities who are above these three, namely, Agni, Vāyu, and Sūrya, the *brahman* made ascend the worlds which are above these three worlds, namely, Pṛthivī, Antarikṣa, and Dyaus. And, indeed, just as these three latter worlds and these three deities are manifest, so too are those higher worlds and the deities, who ascended those worlds, manifest. Then the *brahman* itself went up to the sphere beyond: *atha brahmai 'va parārdham agacchat.*

As for the number of *lokas* or worlds, the *Brāhmaṇa*-texts are by no means unanimous. While they generally speak of the tripartite universe consisting of Pṛthivī, Antarikṣa, and Dyaus, they also contain other enumerations. It, however, needs to be remembered that these different enumerations have very little or no cosmographical significance. They are not intended to illumine the view of the *Brāhmaṇas* regarding the plan and structure of the universe; they are given mainly in order that they should conform to the ritualistic contexts in which they occur. Thus, even the normal tripartite universe is sometimes interpreted (*TBr.* II. 1. [[P183]] 8. 1; *JBr.* I. 154) as being made up of Pitṛ-loka, Devaloka, and Manuṣya-loka. Similarly, while explaining why there have to be two Vedis in the Sautrāmaṇī, the *ŚPBr.* (XII. 7. 3. 7) says: *dvau vāva [[lakāv|lokāv]] ity āhur deva-lokaś cai 'va pitṛ-lokaś ca.* According to the *Aitareya-Āraṇyaka* (II. 4. 1), there are four *lokas*, namely, *ambhas* (which signifies the waters above the heaven), *marīci* (lights or the Dyaus), *mara* (the abode of the mortals, that is, the earth), and *āpaḥ* (waters under the earth). The mention of the earth as *mara* is specially noteworthy and would remind one of what has been already said about the word *kṣam*. In connection with the Puruṣa-medha, the *ŚPBr.* (XIII. 6. 1. 3) mentions the three *lokas*, namely, Pṛthivī, Antarikṣa, and Dyaus, and the Diśaḥ, and adds: *etāvad vā idaṃ sarvaṃ* (that is, presumably, the universe) *yāvad ime ca lokā diśaś ca.* Reference has already been made to the *ṣaḍ urvīḥ*, the six wide ones, mentioned in that *Brāhmaṇa*. Elsewhere that *Brāhmaṇa* (XI. 6. 3. 6) speaks of the universe as having been constituted of eight worlds, which are related to the eight Vasus, namely, Agni, Pṛthivī, Vāyu, Antarikṣa, Āditya, Dyaus, Candramas, and Nakṣatrāṇi.

But the references, which are particularly interesting from the point of view of the evolution of the Hindu cosmography, are those to the seven *lokas*. For, it is these references, which, in a sense, may be said to mark the transition from the Vedic cosmography (which is generally influenced by the number 'three') to the classical Paurāṇic cosmography (which clearly shows the influence of the number 'seven'). The *ŚPBr.* (IX. 5. 2. 8) speaks of the seven worlds of gods merely to make them correspond with a ritualistic detail, namely, the employment of the hymn of seven verses in connection with the piling up of the fire-altar: *saptarcaṃ bhavati | saptacitiko 'gniḥ sapta 'rtavaḥ sapta diśaḥ sapta deva-lokāḥ*, etc. The *Kauṣītaki-Brāhmaṇa* (20. 1) is more specific. It narrates how the gods, mounted on the wheel of the year, moved round the seven *lokas*, namely, the Deva-Pitṛ-loka, the Agni-loka or the Ṛta-dhāma, the Vāyu-loka or the Aparājita (incidentally, it is sometimes suggested that Aparājita denotes the Dyuloka), the Indra-loka or the Adhidiv, the Varuṇa-loka or the Pradiv, the [[P184]] Mṛtyu-loka or the Rocana, and the Brahmaṇo Loka. Altogether, this passage has an unmistakable Paurāṇic ring. More noteworthy, however, is the passage in the *Taittirīya-Āraṇyaka* (X. 10. 1) which speaks of the seven worlds, seven oceans, and seven mountains. In another passage (X. 27. 28), it actually gives the names of the seven worlds; while in a third (I. 7. 1, 3), it even mentions the Mahāmeru. Incidentally, it may be pointed out that, in the context of the adding of fuel to the sacred fires, etc., the *ŚPBr.* (XI. 6. 8–10) alludes to *vanaspatīnāṃ lokaḥ*, *paśūnāṃ lokaḥ*, *oṣadhīnāṃ lokaḥ*, and *apāṃ lokaḥ*, which is reminiscent of the epic-Paurāṇic eschatology. The significance of the plurality of *lokas*, from the point of view of the doctrine of salvation, seems to have been suggested in the *Jaiminīya-Brāhmaṇa* (III. 341 ff.) which tells us how Prajā-pati created the gods; how he wanted to elude them; how, therefore, he went from one *loka* to another; how he stationed the various gods in those *lokas*; and how, finally, he united all those *lokas* into one, called *nāka* or *svarga* or *bradhnasya viṣṭapa*. It becomes clear from this passage that the different *lokas* of the different gods are but partial aspects of the true heaven. In this very context may be mentioned the commentary of the *ŚPBr.* (VI. 3. 1. 17) on the verse in the *Vājasaneyi-Saṃhitā* (11. 5), *śṛṇvantu viśve amṛtasya putrā ā ye dhāmāni divyāni tasthuḥ*: The *amṛta* or the immortal one, verily, is Prajā-pati; his sons are all gods; and the heavenly abodes are these worlds.

Speaking of the universe as a whole, the *ŚPBr.* (XIII. 2.2.13) says that it consists of sixteen parts (*ṣoḍaśa-kalaṃ vā idaṃ sarvam*). As a matter of fact, this division into sixteen parts is applied also to man and the animal. The *lokas* or the worlds are believed to have been founded on the waters and to have been linked with the sun by means of the quarters: *apsu hī 'me lokā pratiṣṭhitāḥ* ... *āditye hī 'me lokā digbhir āsaktāḥ* (*ŚPBr.* VI. 7. 1. 17). It has been further pointed out that the earth is stabilised by means of the mountains and rivers, the *antarikṣa* by means of the birds and *marīci*, and the heaven by means of the clouds and the stars (*ŚPBr.* XI. 8. 1. 2). Elsewhere the entire universe is [[P185]] described as resting on Dyāvā-pṛthivī: *tad ābhyāṃ dyāvā-pṛthivībhyāṃ ārabhate yayor idaṃ sarvam adhi.* (*ŚPBr.* III. 1. 3. 26). The *Kāṇva-ŚPBr.* (IV. 5. 2. 13), however, says that there is nothing beyond Dyāvā-pṛthivī.

A reference has already been made to the Vedic belief that the heaven and the earth were originally joined together. The *Brāhmaṇas* also reiterate that belief. The *ŚPBr.* (I. 4. 1. 22), indeed, says this of all the worlds: *samantikam iva ha vā ime 'gre lokā āsur ity unmṛśyā hai 'va dyaur āsa* "In the beginning, these worlds were well-nigh contiguous to one another; at that time one could touch the heaven thus." But, more specifically about the heaven and the earth, that *Brāhmaṇa* (VII. 1.2.23) says: *saha hai 've 'māv agre lokāv āsatuḥ*; but, in the course of the creation-process, *dyāvā-pṛthivī vyaitām*: the heaven and the earth went asunder (*ŚPBr.* VIII. 4.3.16). And, when they parted asunder, the space, which was between them, became the *antarikṣa*: *saha hai 've 'māv agre lokāv āsatus tayor viyator yo 'ntareṇā ''kāśa āsīt tad antarikṣam abhavat* (*ŚPBr.* VII. 1.2.23). The *Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa* (IV. 27) adds that, after the separation of the heaven and the earth from each other, there was neither rain nor sunshine. The five peoples (*pañca janāḥ*) fell apart. But, eventually, the gods brought about general reconciliation through the marriage of Dyaus and Pṛthivī. The *Taittirīya-Brāhmaṇa* (I. 1. 3. 2), however, has a different, and a more ritual-oriented, version. It says: The heaven and the earth were originally close together. On being separated they said to each other, "Let there be a common sacrificial essence (*yajñiyam*) for us." What sacrificial essence there was belonging to the yonder world, that is, the heaven, that it bestowed on this earth; that became the salty grounds (on the earth). And what sacrificial essence there was belonging to this earth, that it bestowed on the yonder world; that became the black spots in the moon. However, perhaps more illuminating than the above-quoted references to the mutual relationship between the heaven and the earth is the *Brāhmaṇa*-passage, which characterises the two worlds as *retaḥ-sicau*, seed-shedders. It says: Seed-shedders, verily, are these two worlds—this terrestrial world [[P186]] sheds seed upwards from here in the form of smoke; it becomes rain in the yonder world, and that rain the yonder world sheds from above (*ŚPBr.* VII. 4.2.22). Incidentally, it is interesting to note that, in another context, the *Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa* (II. 17) mentions that the heavenly world is at a distance of about 1000 days' travelling on horseback from here, that is, the earth: *sahasram anūcyaṃ svarga-kāmasya sahasrāśvine vā itaḥ svargo lokaḥ*.

As for the shape of Dyaus and Pṛthivī, they are said to be round: *parimaṇḍalau hī 'mau lokau* (*ŚPBr.* VI. 7. 1. 26). [[Elsewhese|Elsewhere]] (*ŚPBr.* VII. 5. 1. 1 ff.), the universe is compared to a tortoise (*sa eṣa [kūrmaḥ] ima eva lokāḥ*). The lower shell of it (*adharaṃ kapālam*) is this terrestrial world; and the upper shell of it is the yonder heaven. For, the yonder heaven has its ends, as it were, bent down (*vyavagṛhītāntā dyauḥ*). And what is between the shells is *antarikṣa*. The cosmographical significance of this passage is unmistakable. The *Brāhmaṇas* also speak of the heaven and the earth as having been properly fitted into each other (cf. *ŚPBr.* VIII. 7. 1. 6).

In the *Brāhmaṇas*, Dyaus is clearly distinguished from Svarga. Broadly speaking, the concept of Svarga is eschatological, while that of Dyaus remains essentially cosmogonical-cosmographical. Svarga is invariably regarded as the ultimate goal of the sacrificial ritual (*svarga-kāmaḥ yajeta*). It would also seem that, as a cosmographical region, Svarga was roughly identified with *nāka* (*ŚPBr.* VIII. 5. 3. 4), which was described as *divaḥ tṛtīyaṃ rocanaṃ pṛṣṭham*: the third luminous back of the heaven (*ŚPBr.* VIII. 6.3.19). In the *Kauṣītaki-Brāhmaṇa* (21. 4; 26. 17), Dyaus is said to be the symbol—the visible symbol—of Svarga. I may, however, add that, in the foregoing discussion about the *Brāhmaṇa*-view of the universe, I have always rendered the word *dyaus* by 'heaven.' As a matter of fact, I have concentrated on Dyaus, more or less ignoring Svarga.

The *Brāhmaṇas* have something interesting to say about the origin of Dyaus. With a desire to multiply, Prajā-pati, by means of Vāyu, entered into union with Antarikṣa; thence an egg arose. [[P187]] From it, yonder Āditya was created. And the juice which was adhering to the shell became the sunbeams (*raśmayaḥ*); and that which was the shell became Dyaus. Similarly, out of the union between Āditya and Dyaus were produced the moon, the intermediate quarters, and the chief quarters (*ŚPBr.* VI. 1. 2. 3–4). Elsewhere (*ŚPBr.* VI. 5. 2. 5), Ādityas are said to have fashioned Dyaus. Dyaus is golden: *hariṇī 'va hi dyauḥ*: (*ŚPBr.* XIV. 1. 3. 29). The close connection between Dyaus and waters is suggested in various ways: Dyaus is described as the seat of waters (*apāṃ sadanam*, *ŚPBr.* VII. 5. 2. 56), or waters are sometimes even identified with Dyaus (*āpo vai dyauḥ*, *ŚPBr.* VI. 4.1.9). Commenting on the *Vājasaneyi-Saṃhitā* (23. 53), the *ŚPBr.* characterises Dyaus as *pūrva-citti* or the first conception. An interesting passage in the *Pañcaviṃśa-Brāhmaṇa* (20. 16. 7) seems to suggest the superiority of Pṛthivī to Dyaus, for, as it points out, the cattle do not procreate in the yonder world. One is here reminded of the view that the earth is ever the bestower of new life, while the heaven is unfruitful—merely a brilliant culmination.

The *Brāhmaṇas* seem to suggest that the earth was originally in a fluid state, and that, eventually, it was solidified through some kind of coagulatory process. The Ādi-varāha or the cosmogonic boar is said to have agitated the primeval waters and to have brought up some mud from the bottom of those waters. It was this mud which became the beginning of the earth (*MS* I. 6. 3). The Ādi-varāha is obviously to be identified with Prajā-pati, and presumably also with the *sūkara* mentioned in the *Atharvaveda* (II. 27. 2; V. 14. 1), and the *varāz* of the *Avesta*. The *Taittirīya-Brāhmaṇa* (I. 1. 3. 5–6) presents a slightly different version of the myth. According to it, nothing was to be seen in the beginning except water and a lotus-leaf standing out above it. Prajā-pati (being bent on creating the firm ground) bethought himself that the lotus-stalk must be resting on something; and, having assumed the form of a boar, he dived and brought up some of the mud. This he spread out (*prath*) on the lotus leaf, whence originated the earth (*pṛthivī*). The *ŚPBr.* (XIV. 1. 2. 11) points [[P188]] out that the earth was, in the beginning, of the size of a span (*prādeśa-mātrī*), that a boar called Emūṣa raised her up, and that this boar was her lord Prajā-pati. The *Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa* says that the earth is round and supportless, but the *ŚPBr.* (VI. 1.2.29) speaks of her being *catuḥ-srakti* (four-cornered) and higher than the waters (*ayaṃ vai lokaḥ adbhyaḥ uttaraḥ*, XIV. 3. 1. 28). This latter *Brāhmaṇa* also speaks (III. 5. 1. 31) of this earth and the other two beyond it (*imāṃ, dve asyāḥ pare*). Finally, from the ritualistic point of view, Pṛthivī is characterised as one syllable of Gāyatrī (*ŚPBr.* VI. 5. 1. 12) and as the great vessel (*mahad āvapanam*, *ŚPBr.* XIII. 2. 6. 13).

All these cosmogonical-cosmographical details from the *Brāhmaṇa*-texts must, however, be regarded as being, in a sense, but minor and incidental. For, the most fundamental concept of the Brāhmaṇic cosmogony is that of the essential bond between sacrifice and the world-process. A reference has already been made to the pan-asuism of the Veda. As a part of this pan-asuistic ideology, the Vedic poet-priests believed that the essential magic potence inhering within a person or a thing could be either augmented or diminished or transferred from one person or thing to another person or thing by means of the magically potent *mantras* and practices—in other words, by means of the sacrificial ritual. The true significance of the magical practices of the *Atharvaveda* and the ritual practices of the *Brāhmaṇas*, indeed, rests on this belief. From the point of view of the subject with which we are now concerned, namely, the universe in Vedic thought, it may be pointed out that the magical potence, *asu*, in the universe dwindles, on account of one reason or another (Prajāpati's creative or procreative functions are said to have drained his powers), and that, as a result of this, the entire cosmic process is seriously jeopardized. Under such circumstances the magical potence in the universe is augmented by means of the sacrificial ritual, and the cosmic process is thereby revived. For instance, on the basis of a critical analysis of the Rāja-sūya, it has been shown[^188_2] that that sacrifice was an [[P189]] annually repeated rite intended for cosmic regeneration and rebirth. It was an abridged representation of the unending cyclical process of decay and regeneration. The Vedic ritualists claimed that whatever was symbolically performed in a sacrifice actually happened in the world-process. Sacrifice can be aptly compared to a clockwork which had to be keyed up in order that the world should be kept going.

Another important doctrine, which developed out of this philosophy of sacrifice, was that of the close correspondence between macrocosm and microcosm. It is, indeed, on the basis of this doctrine that the *Brāhmaṇa*-authors set many of their important ideas into the framework of the five "correspondences" (*pañcasu adhikaraṇeṣu*).

As has been indicated earlier, the various *lokas* or worlds can be considered from three main points of view, namely, (1) as so many regions of the universe, (2) as the special abodes of particular gods, and (3) as rewards for meritorious deeds (or punishments for sins). In popular Hindu thought, these three points of view are seen to have been actually combined to make up a single religious complex. The Vedic poets, on the other hand, do not seem to have established any definite correlation between their cosmography and eschatology. Popular Hinduism may be said to be rather excessively obsessed by the ideas of after-life and *svarga-naraka*. *Svarga*, the heaven, had then come to be clearly distinguished from *dyaus*, the sky. In the *Ṛg-veda*, we have only *dyaus* (the word *svarga* occurs in the *Ṛg-veda* only once in X. 95. 18),[^189_3] and it possesses an exclusively cosmographical significance. It may be suggested that the cheerful, life-affirming, buoyant outlook on life of the Ṛgvedic people—their concern for a happy, secure, prosperous family-life "here and now"—made them averse to thinking overmuch about the after-life and the felicity in the other world. Accordingly, they did not develop any substantial eschatological speculations.

At the time of the interment of the bones of the dead man, he is asked to "crouch towards this mother earth, the spacious and friendly earth", for, "the young lady, soft as wool to the giver of *dakṣiṇā*", will "guard you against the lap of *nirṛti*" (*upa sarpa mātaraṃ bhūmim etām uru-vyacasaṃ pṛthivīṃ suśevām | ūrṇa-mradā yuvatir dakṣiṇāvata eṣā tvā pātu nirṛter upasthāt ||* *ṚV* X. 18. 10). One may see in these references a cosmographical region having been somehow related to the post-mortem condition of man. The notions that the various constituents of the universe have originated from the different limbs of the cosmic Puruṣa,[^190_4] that there exists a close correspondence between the macrocosm and the microcosm, and that, therefore, the different limbs of a person go back after his death to the corresponding constituents of the universe, seem to form the ideological basis of such passages as *ṚV* X. 16. 3: *sūryaṃ cakṣur gacchatu vātam ātmā dyāṃ ca gaccha pṛthivīṃ ca dharmaṇā*. It must be added that the word *dharmaṇā*, in this context, has to be understood in the sense of 'in accordance with the natural correspondence' and not of 'in accordance with the religious merit.'

The Ṛgvedic poets do speak of a land (or lands) of felicity where the god-devoted people are described to be enjoying themselves (for instance, *yatrā naro devayanto yugāni vitanvate prati bhadrāya bhadram*, *ṚV* I. 115. 2; or *naro yatra devayavo madanti*, *ṚV* I. 154. 5), but they do so not necessarily in any eschatological contexts. Nor do they identify those lands with any cosmographical regions.[^190_5] A direct juxtaposition of the concept of the [[P191]] world presided over by a particular god (which, however, is not sought to be identified with any cosmographical region) and the eschatological speculation is to be found in such passages as *ṚV* X. 14. 8. *ṚV* X. 14. 8 reads: *saṃ gacchasva pitṛbhiḥ saṃ yamene 'ṣṭāpūrtena parame vyoman | hitvāyā 'vadyaṃ punar astam ehi saṃ gacchasva tanvā suvarcāḥ* (I would read *suvarcasā* for *suvarcāḥ*). A critical study of this stanza would bring out the following significant points: After death, a person goes to the world of Yama. This world of Yama (*yamaloka*) is also the world where the Pitars live (*pitṛbhiḥ yamena saṃ gacchasva*). In other words, the *yamaloka* is identical with the *pitṛ-loka*.[^191_6] It is located in the highest heaven (*parame vyoman*). A person attains to this world only as the result of his *iṣṭāpūrta*—of his acts of piety and charity. This world is, indeed, man's own natural home (*asta*). Before reaching there, a person leaves behind all that was reprehensible in him (*avadyaṃ hitvāya*), and assumes a new resplendent form (*suvarcasā tanvā*). It may be pointed out that this Ṛgvedic passage corresponds almost fully with *AV* VI. 120. 3, which reads: *yatrā suhārdaḥ sukṛto madanti vihāya rogaṃ tanvaḥ svāyāḥ | aśloṇā aṅgair ahrutāḥ svarge tatra paśyema pitarau ca putrān*. The most noteworthy difference between the two passages, however, is that instead of the *yamaloka* implied in the Ṛgvedic passage, the Atharvavedic passage unequivocally speaks of *svarga*. The points of correspondence between the two passages are: (*ṚV*) *pitṛbhiḥ saṃ gacchasva* : (*AV*) *pitarau ca putrān paśyema*; (*ṚV*) *iṣṭāpūrtena* : (*AV*) *sukṛtaḥ*; (*ṚV*) *avadyaṃ hitvāya* : (*AV*) *svāyāḥ tanvaḥ rogaṃ vihāya* : and (*ṚV*) *suvarcasā tanvā* : (*AV*) *aśloṇāḥ aṅgaiḥ ahrutāḥ*. Allusions in the Ṛg-veda to hell as such are almost nil, and the notion in respect of it is noticeably vague. But the idea that the sinners and evil-doers are condemned to suffer in some dismal abyss occurs not infrequently. In one Ṛgvedic passage (IV. 5. 5), for instance, the sinful, crooked, and untruthful are said to have prepared for themselves a *gabhīra pada*, a deep station: *pāpāsaḥ santo anṛtā asatyā idaṃ padam ajanatā gabhīram*. Sāyaṇa, in his commentary on the passage, understands the phrase, *gabhīra pada*, as denoting some infernal region or hell; GELDNER translates *gabhīra pada* with 'geheimnisvolles Wort',[^193_19] but Sāyaṇa's interpretation seems to be confirmed by the imprecation in IV. 5. 14d (*anāyudhāsa ā asatā sacantām*) where the word *asat* is used more or less as a synonym of *nirṛti* which latter word also means perdition. It may be, incidentally, pointed out, in this context, that, according to W. NORMAN BROWN,[^193_20] *asat* is the term by which hell is denoted in the Ṛg-veda. Hell represents just the opposite of the well-ordered world which latter is denoted by the word *sat*. Thus, hell is regarded as being similar to the primordial chaos. At the beginning of the series of these lectures, I had stated that man, universe, and god were generally regarded as the three main themes of man's religio-philosophical speculations. In connection with these three, the German philosopher, FEUERBACH, has said: "God is my first thought, world my next, and man my third and last." In their religio-philosophical quest, the Hindu thinkers may be said to have travelled just the other way round. Their starting point was man. But this did not result in their thought-complex becoming anthropocentric. They did not claim, like Protagoras, that man was the measure of all things. Hindu ideology has always been essentially cosmic. It is said that the three ancient thought-worlds had their respective watchwords. The watchword of the ancient Western thought was "I" (*aham*); that of the ancient Chinese thought was "we" (*vayam*); and that of the ancient Indian thought was "all" (*viśvam*). Hindu thinkers have no doubt thought of *aham*—but they have thought of *aham* as "a part of *viśvam*" and not as "apart from *viśvam*."
