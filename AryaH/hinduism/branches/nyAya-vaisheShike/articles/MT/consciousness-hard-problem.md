## Intro
After all the hard problem has more of a philosophical implication: if you ask an average person is another man conscious, they will say yes. If you next ask them is a fly is conscious then some would agree it is. If you as them if a plant is conscious even fewer might. If you ask them a stone is conscious most might say no. Now if you ask them the reason for their responses then some might invoke some aspect of biology. 

If that intuition is right then you have to ask why that is so? The more you probe people in this regard you find that they have difficulty presenting a completely coherent model for "emergence" of this property of consciousness -- there is a leap of faith at some point where it is equated to **computational self-awareness**+++(5)+++ -- i.e., a system that has a feedback loop in the computations it performs should be conscious. However, there is no logical bridge between this and qualia -- the first person experience. Hence a hard problem.

## LLM 
Source: [TW](https://x.com/blog_supplement/status/2112699444634243180)

LLMs cut both ways with respect to what in the Occident is termed the "Hard problem of Consciousness." H did not see it as much of a hard problem, despite having some of the same positions as have emerged in the Neo-Occident.  They more or less took it as axiomatic. 

There were:  
flavors of philosophical dualism (sAMkhyA, yoga, one could interpret vaisheShika that way);  
consciousness-only monism (uttara-mIMAMsa as interpreted by advaita);  
matter-only monism with consciousness as emergent (chArvAka);  
pan-psychism (certain other interpretations of uttara-mImAMsA). 

Barring the chArvaka-s, all accepted consciousness as a real thing and did not see it as the thing needing philosophical explanation. 

Then there is the whole branch of "process dualism or pluralism" (Several bauddha schools).  
While these reject independent existence of consciousness,  
they are not matter-only monists like the chArvAka;  
instead, they posit its existence through a continuous interactive process.  
Even, for them, it is to a degree an axiomatic element of the co-dependent process.

One could look at LLMs and say that in them we get a perfect instantiation of Chalmers' philosophical zombies. It shows a system (like an organism) can function perfectly well as a person without being conscious (as some LLMs say they are not). 

Hence, the hard problem: why is there consciousness? On the other hand, a substance-only monist would look at them and say that they illustrate why the sense of being conscious is a mere illusion. They seem very conscious, but there is really nothing there. If one broadly accepts the sAMkhyA framework, one could say there is a way out of this, but then people would ask: why should one accept the sAMkhyA framework?
