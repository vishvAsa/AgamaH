## Leaving the temple
The Gospels and Book of Acts provide a narrative of God's presence leaving the temple in Jerusalem. You have the tearing of the veil of the Holy of Holies within the temple mount upon Christ's crucifixion, and later Paul's remark that God will no longer inhabit manmade temples. 

## New covenant?
It is interpreted several ways, including symbolising the end of the old covenant with the Jews and God opening himself up to the world in the new covenant established by Christ, and sometimes also that God's spirit exited the temple to 'prepare the way' for the apostles. 

## Roman evocatio
Without making any commentary on the particulars of this account, I wanted to present a curious primary source contradiction instead. I believe I shared this excerpt before from the same article, but it's only now occurred to me how the account conflicts with the NT books.

For context, an 'evocatio' was a ritual that the Romans would perform when invading a foreign land or laying siege against a foreign city. It was an offering given to foreign deities to "convince" them to relinquish their protection of their enemies and to instead favour Rome. 

In this case, the besieging Romans performed an evocatio to 'the gods of the Hebrews', i.e., Yahweh, so that Jerusalem would lose his protection. In the Bible, the presence of Yahweh was said to have quite literally inhabited the Temple Mount within its inner Holy of Holies. 

What makes this account extremely curious is in how Tacitus, a Roman author, and Josephus, a Jewish author, both separately agreed upon the facts and occurrence of the event. 

The only difference is Josephus interpreted it to mean Yahweh had went into the world to prepare the way for the Jewish diaspora, whereas Tacitus - aware of its correlation with the Roman evocatio - connects it with the Jewish deity having abandoned his protection of the city. 

Considering the two accounts were given in total separation from one another, that the authors were of diametric cultures at war, and that both were alive at the time of the event with Josephus himself actually present at the siege, its historicity to me seems very likely. 

## Post-jesus
However, this siege of Jerusalem was in 70 AD- several decades after the crucifixion of Jesus. If the tearing of the veil occurred, and God's presence had exited the temple as Paul claims in Acts, then whom was inhabiting the Holy of Holies until the Roman evocatio? 

If replied: "Perhaps a demon was in the temple after God left to fool the Jews into giving it worship", consider the words of Jesus in Matthew 12:26, "If Satan casts out Satan, he is divided against himself. How then will his kingdom stand?"

How could the evocatio cast it out? 
Contrary to the Christian account of God's presence having left the Holy of Holies, it seems instead that the guardian spirit the Jews revered as the one true god continued to inhabit the Temple Mount decades after the crucifixion of Jesus, his death being inconsequential to it. 
It was common in the Near East to worship a guardian god of the city. More often than not, cults to the city's tutelary deity developed into strong monolatry, or like in the case of Babylon's city god, Marduk, they're elevated to replace the chief deity of their religion. 
Jerusalem was no different. Even the Bible describes that it was common for worship to be given to many gods of the Canaanite pantheon in Judea, but
a belligerent form of monolatry developed that insisted all worship be directed to the ethnic tutelary guardian, Yahweh. 
Yahweh initially had adopted the role of El in the Canaanite pantheon, he is even still referred to as El in the Bible. He also took the name Baal, which also appears in the Bible. His assumption into becoming El meant also inheriting his consort, Asherah. 
Radical dedication to the god would eventually evolve into total and exclusive monotheism, a position likely spurred both by anxiety they were not dedicated enough to receive his protection given Jerusalem's repeated subjugations, and by an intent to consolidate political power. 
However, despite the evolution of what Judeans believed their god to be, the god remained the same. The spirit inhabiting the Holy of Holies remained but a local daemon, thus still answerable to the forces that could compel it. Hence upon the Roman evocatio, 'God' fled Jerusalem.
