Euhemerus : Gods were once men

Around 300 BC, the Greek writer Euhemerus of Messene composed a work called the Sacred Inscription (Hiera Anagraphe), which is a fictional travelogue describing a voyage to the utopian island of Panchaea in the Indian Ocean. There, Euhemerus claimed to have found a golden column inscribed with the “true” history of Zeus, Uranus, Cronus, and the other so called Olympian gods which were worshipped by his fellow Greeks who lived on that island.

Euhemerus was arguing that the Greek gods of Olympus were not deities but flesh-and-blood kings, and conquerors of a very early age whose memory only vaguely remained in his day. He is said to have proposed that the grateful (or fearful) subjects of these historic kings (Zeus, Uranus, etc…) deified them after death and that their exploits were later exaggerated over the course of time to become the fabulous myths described by poets like Homer and Hesiod. Zeus was just a mortal king of Crete who traveled the world subduing enemies and establishing kingdoms for his friends and relatives, and who was buried on Crete, where later ages showed his tomb.

The original Greek text of Euhemerus does not survive unfortunately ; its existence and fragments of it are known mainly through the Latin translation and paraphrase made by the later poet Ennius (2nd century BC), which also in turn survives now largely because the Christian writer Lactantius quotes it at length in his work Divine Institutes.

In the pagan world itself, Euhemerus’s reception was mostly hostile. To orthodox pagan religious sensibility, his theory felt like utter impiety dressed up as historical scholarship, and ancient sources report that he was branded an atheist alongside other figures. For example, the famous poet Callimachus (and others) attacked Euhemerus as an impious fraud and blasphemous liar who had slandered Zeus and daring to claim that he had found his tomb. The Stoic philosophers too, for their part, mostly rejected the crude literal historicism of the theory, while preferring allegorical explanations of the gods over historical ones.

But, the very fact that the work of the pagan Euhemerus survives today because it is chiefly embedded inside Christian polemic against paganism must tell us that how useful his fringe theory was to Christians. Christian writers (as we will see from the next section onwards) admiringly note that Euhemerus and men like him were mocked with a name that, in their view, they didn’t fully deserve, because pagans like Euhemerus had at least seen through popular error even if they hadn’t reached the full truth of the Gospel of the one true God. For example, the church father Clement of Alexandria who lived in the second century in his work Exhortation against the Pagans, writes the following (source link here) :

All this – for I must not in the least conceal what I think – makes me amazed how the term atheist has been applied to Euhemerus of Acragas, Nicanor of Cyprus, Diagoras and Hippo of Melos, with that Cyrenian named Theodorus and a good many others besides, men who lived sensible lives and discerned more acutely, I imagine, than the rest of mankind the error connected with these gods. Even if they did not perceive the truth itself, they at least suspected the error; and this suspicion is a living spark of wisdom, and no small one, which grows up like a seed into truth.

The Patristic Period

Confronted with a Greco-Roman world saturated with extremely ancient gods and methods of worship, the early Christian writers needed additional explanations about how they originated and why they became so entrenched. It was not enough to simply call paganism false and demonically inspired. Simple denial (”there are no gods at all, this is empty superstition”) risked lumping Christians in with “atheists”. Also, this mere denial could not account for why the pagan gods had temples, priesthoods, festivals, statues, and (most inconveniently for the Christian apologists), apparent oracles, miracles, and answered prayers. Euhemerism solved this. If the gods were once real men (kings, benefactors, culture-heroes, occasionally scoundrels), then their existence could be conceded (they really lived, they really did things) while their divinity was demolished (they are dead, mortal, and therefore not eligible for worship). It had a second advantage: it let Christian writers claim to out-history the pagans on their own turf, using the pagans’ own antiquarian scholarship against pagan religion. And it had a third advantage that would still be active a millennium and a half later in locations as far as Iceland and Peru: it let Christian scribes and missionaries preserve, transcribe, and even enjoy pre-Christian gods, myths, and epics, without approving it as religion. The myths of the old gods could be kept as history and literature once they had been safely demoted to dead human ancestors whose adventures lay in the realm of history and not religion. What follows traces this Christian maneuver, that is the “your gods are just historical men” argument — through the patristic period, into the medieval world (Christian Scandinavia particularly), and finally into the Spanish and English colonisation of the Americas.

3.1 Athenagoras of Athens (c. 177 AD)

One of the earliest and most explicit statements of Euhemerism comes from the church father Athenagoras’s work Legatio pro Christianis (“A Plea for the Christians”), addressed to the Roman emperors Marcus Aurelius and Commodus. Chapter 28 of this plea is titled, in the standard English edition, “The Heathen Gods Were Simply Men.” Athenagoras attributes the invention of these cults to demonic deception utilising real historical memory. He explains that the demons who draw men to idols feed on the blood of sacrifices, but insists that the gods who please the crowd, whose names are attached to the images, were men. The fact that these gods are men can be learned from their history, as per him. He misquotes many ancient pagan figures to support his view. He runs through a catalogue of examples of local heroisation drawn straight from Greek civic religion : for examples - the people of Ilium worshipping the hero Hector as a god, the Spartans venerating the hero Agamemnon as Zeus, the man of Tenedos worshipping Tennes, the Athenians sacrificing to Erechtheus as Poseidon. He notes that every nation and people offers whatever sacrifices and celebrates whatever mysteries it pleases. The full chapter is quoted below (source link here)

But it is perhaps necessary, in accordance with what has already been adduced, to say a little about their names. Herodotus, then, and Alexander the son of Philip, in his letter to his mother (and each of them is said to have conversed with the priests at Heliopolis, and Memphis, and Thebes), affirm that they learnt from them that the gods had been men. Herodotus speaks thus: “Of such a nature were, they said, the beings represented by these images, they were very far indeed from being gods. However, in the times anterior to them it was otherwise; then Egypt had gods for its rulers, who dwelt upon the earth with men, one being always supreme above the rest. The last of these was Horus the son of Osiris, called by the Greeks Apollo. He deposed Typhon, and ruled over Egypt as its last god-king. Osiris is named Dionysus (Bacchus) by the Greeks.” “Almost all the names of the gods came into Greece from Egypt.” Apollo was the son of Dionysus and Isis, as He rodotus likewise affirms: “According to the Egyptians, Apollo and Diana are the children of Bacchus and Isis; while Latona is their nurse and their preserver.” These beings of heavenly origin they had for their first kings: partly from ignorance of the true worship of the Deity, partly from gratitude for their government, they esteemed them as gods together with their wives. “The male kine, if clean, and the male calves are used for sacrifice by the Egyptians universally; but the females, they are not allowed to sacrifice, since they are sacred to Isis. The statue of this goddess has the form of a woman but with horns like a cow, resembling those of the Greek representations of Io.” And who can be more deserving of credit in making these statements, than those who in family succession, son from father, received not only the priesthood, but also the history? For it is not likely that the priests, who make it their business to commend the idols to men’s reverence, would assert falsely that they were men. If Herodotus alone had said that the Egyptians spoke in their histories of the gods as of men, when he says, “What they told me concerning their religion it is not my intention to repeat, except only the names of their deities, things of very trifling importance,” it would behove us not to credit even Herodotus as being a fabulist. But as Alexander and Hermes surnamed Trismegistus, who shares with them in the attribute of eternity, and innumerable others, not to name them individually, [declare the same], no room is left even for doubt that they, being kings, were esteemed gods. That they were men, the most learned of the Egyptians also testify, who, while saying that ether, earth, sun, moon, are gods, regard the rest as mortal men, and the temples as their sepulchres (graveyards).

Apollodorus, too, asserts the same thing in his treatise concerning the gods.

But Herodotus calls even their sufferings mysteries. “The ceremonies at the feast of Isis in the city of Busiris have been already spoken of. It is there that the whole multitude, both of men and women, many thousands in number, beat them selves at the close of the sacrifice in honour of a god whose name a religious scruple forbids me to mention.” If they are gods, they are also immortal; but if people are beaten for them, and their sufferings are mysteries, they are men, as Herodotus himself says: “Here, too, in this same precinct of Minerva at Sais, is the burial-place of one whom I think it not right to mention in such a connection. It stands behind the temple against the back wall, which it entirely covers. There are also some large stone obelisks in the enclosure, and there is a lake near them, adorned with an edging of stone. In form it is circular, and in size, as it seemed to me, about equal to the lake at Delos called the Hoop. On this lake it is that the Egyptians represent by night his sufferings whose name I refrain from mentioning, and this representation they call their mysteries.” And not only is the sepulchre of Osiris shown, but also his embalming: “When a body is brought to them, they show the bearer various models of corpses made in wood, and painted so as to resemble nature. The most perfect is said to be after the manner of him whom I do not think it religious to name in connection with such a matter.”

But among the Greeks, also, those who are eminent in poetry and history say the same thing. Thus of Heracles:- “That lawless wretch, that man of brutal strength, Deaf to Heaven’s voice, the social rite transgressed.” Such being his nature, deservedly did he go mad, and deservedly did he light the funeral pile and burn himself to death. Of Asklepius, Hesiod says:- “The mighty father both of gods and men Was filled with wrath, and from Olympus’ top With flaming thunderbolt cast down and slew Latona’s well-loved son–such was his ire.”

And Pindar:- “But even wisdom is ensnared by gain. The brilliant bribe of gold seen in the hand Ev’n him perverted: therefore Kronos’ son With both hands quickly stopp’d his vital breath, And by a bolt of fire ensured his doom’. Either, therefore, they were gods and did not hanker after gold- “O gold, the fairest prize to mortal men, Which neither mother equals in delight, Nor children dear” - for the Deity is in want of nought, and is superior to carnal desire, nor did they die; or, having been born men, they were wicked by reason of ignorance, and overcome by love of money. What more need I say, or refer to Castor, or Pollux, or Amphiaraus, who, having been born, so to speak, only the other day, men of men, are looked upon as gods, when they imagine even Ino after her madness and its consequent sufferings to have become a goddess “Sea-rovers will her name Leucothea.” And her son:- “August Palaemon, sailors will invoke.”

For if detestable and god-hated men had the reputation of being gods, and the daughter of Derceto, Semiramis, a lascivious and blood-stained woman, was esteemed a Syria goddess; and if, on account of Derceto, the Syrians worship doves and Semiramis (for, a thing impossible, a woman was changed into a dove: the story is in Ctesias), what wonder if some should be called gods by their people on the ground of their rule and sovereignty (the Sibyl, of whom Plato also makes mention, says:- “It was the generation then the tenth, Of men endowed with speech, since forth the flood Had burst upon the men of former times, And Kronos, Japetus, and Titan reigned, Whom men, of Ouranos and Gaia Proclaimed the noblest sons, and named them so; Because of men endowed with gift of speech, They were the first”; and others for their strength, as Heracles and Perseus; and others for their art, as Asclepius? Those, therefore, to whom either the subjects gave honour or the rulers themselves [assumed it], obtained the name, some from fear, others from revenge. Thus Antinous, through the benevolence of your ancestors towards their subjects, came to be regarded as a god. But those who came after adopted the worship without examination.

“The Cretans always lie; for they, O king, Have built a tomb to thee who art not dead.” Though you believe, O Callimachus, in the nativity of Zeus, you do not believe in his sepulchre; and whilst you think to obscure the truth, you in fact proclaim him dead, even to those who are ignorant; and if you see the cave, you call to mind the childbirth of Rhea; but when you see the coffin, you throw a shadow over his death, not considering that the unbegotten God alone is eternal. For either the tales told by the multitude and the poets about the gods are unworthy of credit, and the reverence shown them is superfluous (for those do not exist, the tales concerning whom are untrue); or if the births, the amours, the murders, the thefts, the castrations, the thunderbolts, are true, they no longer exist, having ceased to be since they were born, having previously had no being. And on what principle must we believe some things and disbelieve others, when the poets have written their stories in order to gain greater veneration for them? For surely those through whom they have got to be considered gods, and who have striven to represent their deeds as worthy of reverence, cannot have invented their sufferings. That, therefore, we are not atheists, acknowledging as we do God the Maker of this universe and His Logos, has been proved according to my ability, if not according to the importance of the subject.

Clement of Alexandria (c. 195 AD)

We have already been introduced to this figure in the previous section where he claimed that thinkers like Euhemerus were farther apart than any heathens of their day in seeing the error of the truth claims of pagan religion. He provides a systematic account of how pagan cults arose and how Greek religion developed in stages, from the worship of heavenly bodies, through the worship of the fruits of agriculture (giving rise to the cults of Demeter and Dionysus), down to outright hero-cult of dead men. This surprisingly sounds very familiar and resonant with the modern secular historians of religion and anthropologists as well !

Tertullian (c. 197 AD)

The church father Tertullian makes the argument almost as a rhetorical trap in his work Apologeticus where he argues that : since pagans themselves admit their gods were once ordinary mortals, on what grounds did those mortals ever become divine? He points out that Christians do not worship men, yet pagans themselves admit that their gods were once merely that, and he also asks how they became gods and what they did to earn divinity, when they certainly did not make the world or anything in it. He says that one reason why a human like Saturn was eventually deified to become considered a god was as follows. Because the memories of the human lineage of the human Saturn (like who his human father was) had faded with time, people began to consider him as a son of the heaven and the earth, as a way of veneration and honour. He says that even common folk call persons whose stock is unknown as sons of earth and this is what had happened that led to the deification of Saturn as a god. He also supports his argument by saying that even today, people make gods of living and dead humans (familial ancestor worship and veneration of kings and emperors). So, all pagan gods were humans who started out as being venerated for their deeds and over the passage of time with lapse of memory, their origins became conflated with the supernatural and they became deified. He concludes by saying “But I pass from these remarks, for I know and I am going to show what your gods are not, by showing what they are. In reference, then, to these (gods), I see only names of dead men of ancient times; I hear fabulous stories; I recognise sacred rites founded on mere myths. As to the actual images, I regard them as simply pieces of matter akin to the vessels and utensils in common use among us”. If you want to read more of his original words, some extracts from Chapter 10 onwards follows (source link here)

But you say, They are gods. We protest and appeal from yourselves to your knowledge; let that judge us; let that condemn us, if it can deny that all these gods of yours were but men. If even it venture to deny that, it will be confuted by its own books of antiquities, from which it has got its information about them, bearing witness to this day, as they plainly do, both of the cities in which they were born, and the countries in which they have left traces of their exploits, as well as where also they are proved to have been buried. Shall I now, therefore, go over them one by one, so numerous and so various, new and old, barbarian, Grecian, Roman, foreign, captive and adopted, private and common, male and female, rural and urban, naval and military? It were useless even to hunt out all their names: so I may content myself with a compend; and this not for your information, but that you may have what you know brought to your recollection, for undoubtedly you act as if you had forgotten all about them. No one of your gods is earlier than Saturn: from him you trace all your deities, even those of higher rank and better known. What, then, can be proved of the first, will apply to those that follow. So far, then, as books give us information, neither the Greek Diodorus or Thallus, neither Cassius Severus or Cornelius Nepos, nor any writer upon sacred antiquities, have ventured to say that Saturn was any but a man: so far as the question depends on facts, I find none more trustworthy than those— that in Italy itself we have the country in which, after many expeditions, and after having partaken of Attic hospitalities, Saturn settled, obtaining cordial welcome from Janus, or, as the Salii will have it, Janis. The mountain on which he dwelt was called Saturnius; the city he founded is called Saturnia to this day; last of all, the whole of Italy, after having borne the name of Oenotria, was called Saturnia from him. He first gave you the art of writing, and a stamped coinage, and thence it is he presides over the public treasury. But if Saturn were a man, he had undoubtedly a human origin; and having a human origin, he was not the offspring of heaven and earth. As his parents were unknown, it was not unnatural that he should be spoken of as the son of those elements from which we might all seem to spring. For who does not speak of heaven and earth as father and mother, in a sort of way of veneration and honour? Or from the custom which prevails among us of saying that persons of whom we have no knowledge, or who make a sudden appearance, have fallen from the skies? In this way it came about that Saturn, everywhere a sudden and unlooked-for , got everywhere the name of the Heaven-born. For even the common folk call persons whose stock is unknown, sons of earth. I say nothing of how men in these rude times were wont to act, when they were impressed by the look of any stranger happening to appear among them, as though it were divine, since even at this day, men of culture make gods of those whom, a day or two before, they acknowledged to be dead men by their public mourning for them. Let these notices of Saturn, brief as they are, suffice. It will thus also be proved that Jupiter is as certainly a man, as from a man he sprung; and that one after another the whole swarm (of gods) is mortal like the primal stock.

And since, as you dare not deny that these deities of yours once were men, you have taken it on you to assert that they were made gods after their decease, let us consider what necessity there was for this. In the first place, you must concede the existence of one higher God — a certain wholesale dealer in divinity, who has made gods of men. For they could neither have assumed a divinity which was not theirs, nor could any but one himself possessing it have conferred it on them. If there was no one to make gods, it is vain to dream of gods being made when thus you have no god-maker. Most certainly, if they could have deified themselves, with a higher state at their command, they never would have been men. If, then, there be one who is able to make gods, I turn back to an examination of any reason there may be for making gods at all; and I find no other reason than this, that the great God has need of their ministrations and aids in performing the offices of Deity. But first it is an unworthy idea that He should need the help of a man, and in fact a dead man, when, if He was to be in want of this assistance from the dead, He might more fittingly have created some one a god at the beginning. Nor do I see any place for his action. For this entire world-mass — whether self-existent and uncreated, as Pythagoras maintains, or brought into being by a creator’s hands, as Plato holds — was manifestly, once for all in its original construction, disposed, and furnished, and ordered, and supplied with a government of perfect wisdom. That cannot be imperfect which has made all perfect. There was nothing waiting on for Saturn and his race to do. Men will make fools of themselves if they refuse to believe that from the very first rain poured down from the sky, and stars gleamed, and light shone, and thunders roared, and Jove himself dreaded the lightnings you put in his hands; that in like manner before Bacchus, and Ceres, and Minerva, nay before the first man, whoever that was, every kind of fruit burst forth plentifully from the bosom of the earth, for nothing provided for the support and sustenance of man could be introduced after his entrance on the stage of being. Accordingly, these necessaries of life are said to have been discovered, not created. But the thing you discover existed before; and that which had a pre-existence must be regarded as belonging not to him who discovered it, but to him who made it, for of course it had a being before it could be found. But if, on account of his being the discoverer of the vine, Bacchus is raised to godship, Lucullus, who first introduced the cherry from Pontus into Italy, has not been fairly dealt with; for as the discoverer of a new fruit, he has not, as though he were its creator, been awarded divine honours. Wherefore, if the universe existed from the beginning, thoroughly furnished with its system working under certain laws for the performance of its functions, there is, in this respect, an entire absence of all reason for electing humanity to divinity; for the positions and powers which you have assigned to your deities have been from the beginning precisely what they would have been, although you had never deified them. But you turn to another reason, telling us that the conferring of deity was a way of rewarding worth. And hence you grant, I conclude, that the god-making God is of transcendent righteousness — one who will neither rashly, improperly, nor needlessly bestow a reward so great. I would have you then consider whether the merits of your deities are of a kind to have raised them to the heavens, and not rather to have sunk them down into lowest depths of Tartarus — the place which you regard, with many, as the prison-house of infernal punishments. For into this dread place are wont to be cast all who offend against filial piety, and such as are guilty of incest with sisters, and seducers of wives, and ravishers of virgins, and boy-polluters, and men of furious tempers, and murderers, and thieves, and deceivers; all, in short, who tread in the footsteps of your gods, not one of whom you can prove free from crime or vice, save by denying that they had ever a human existence. But as you cannot deny that, you have those foul blots also as an added reason for not believing that they were made gods afterwards. For if you rule for the very purpose of punishing such deeds; if every virtuous man among you rejects all correspondence, converse, and intimacy with the wicked and base, while, on the other hand, the high God has taken up their mates to a share of His majesty, on what ground is it that you thus condemn those whose fellow-actors you adore? Your goodness is an affront in the heavens. Deify your vilest criminals, if you would please your gods. You honour them by giving divine honours to their fellows. But to say no more about a way of acting so unworthy, there have been men virtuous, and pure, and good. Yet how many of these nobler men you have left in the regions of doom! As Socrates, so renowned for his wisdom, Aristides for his justice, Themistocles for his warlike genius, Alexander for his sublimity of soul, Polycrates for his good fortune, Crœsus for his wealth, Demosthenes for his eloquence. Which of these gods of yours is more remarkable for gravity and wisdom than Cato, more just and warlike than Scipio? Which of them more magnanimous than Pompey, more prosperous than Sylla, of greater wealth than Crassus, more eloquent than Tullius? How much better it would have been for the God Supreme to have waited that He might have taken such men as these to be His heavenly associates, prescient as He must have surely been of their worthier character! He was in a hurry, I suppose, and straightway shut heaven’s gates; and now He must surely feel ashamed at these worthies murmuring over their lot in the regions below.

But I pass from these remarks, for I know and I am going to show what your gods are not, by showing what they are. In reference, then, to these, I see only names of dead men of ancient times; I hear fabulous stories; I recognise sacred rites founded on mere myths. As to the actual images, I regard them as simply pieces of matter akin to the vessels and utensils in common use among us, or even undergoing in their consecration a hapless change from these useful articles at the hands of reckless art, which in the transforming process treats them with utter contempt, nay, in the very act commits sacrilege; so that it might be no slight solace to us in all our punishments, suffering as we do because of these same gods, that in their making they suffer as we do themselves.

In his earlier and cruder companion work, Ad Nationes (To the Nations), Tertullian goes further and multiplies concrete historical identifications. He treats the pagan gods as simply deified men and even claims that the Egyptian god Serapis was derived from the biblical character of Joseph and notes that Antinous, the lover of the Roman Emperor Hadrian had been deified within living memory.

In Chapter 8, he claims that since different peoples of different nations worship different gods, these religions are a matter of local customs and local culture, rather than universal truth.

Does this hut so familiarly hard to you?!!! Demoting pagan gods as local ancestors and customs associated with them as matters of local culture while denying the religiosities claimed for them is not a modern strategy but one that has been with the Church right from its inception. In Chapter 12 , he again claims that the Roman god Saturn was originally a human and that conflicting stories about the lives and identities of these figures revered as gods (local variations in their myths) further undermine their claim to divinity. In Chapters 14 and 16, he claims that deified humans such as Hercules and Dionysus and also all other gods (who according to him have been deified humans in distant time) may have performed notable deeds, but extraordinary achievements do not justify worship because human excellence should never be confused with divine nature. Chapter 8

There remains the gentile class of gods among the several nations: these were adopted out of mere caprice, not from the knowledge of the truth; and our information about them comes from the private notions of different races. God (the Christian universal one), I imagine, is everywhere known, everywhere present, powerful everywhere — an object whom all ought to worship, all ought to serve. Since, then, it happens that even they, whom all the world worships in common, fail in the evidence of their true divinity, how much more must this befall those whom their very votaries have not succeeded in discovering! For what useful authority could possibly precede a theology of so defective a character as to be wholly unknown to fame? How many have either seen or heard of the Syrian Atargatis, the African Cœlestis, the Moorish Varsutina, the Arabian Obodas and Dusaris, or the Norican Belenus, or those whom Varro mentions — Deluentinus of Casinum, Visidianus of Narnia, Numiternus of Atina, or Ancharia of Asculum? And who have any clear notions of Nortia of Vulsinii? There is no difference in the worth of even their names, apart from the human surnames which distinguish them. I laugh often enough at the little coteries of gods in each municipality, which have their honours confined within their own city walls. To what lengths this licence of adopting gods has been pushed, the superstitious practices of the Egyptians show us; for they worship even their native animals, such as cats, crocodiles, and their snake. It is therefore a small matter that they have also deified a man — him, I mean, whom not Egypt only, or Greece, but the whole world worships, and the Africans swear by; about whose state also all that helps our conjectures and imparts to our knowledge the semblance of truth is stated in our own (sacred) literature. For that Serapis of yours was originally one of our own saints called Joseph. The youngest of his brethren, but superior to them in intellect, he was from envy sold into Egypt, and became a slave in the family of Pharaoh king of the country. Importuned by the unchaste queen, when he refused to comply with her desire, she turned upon him and reported him to the king, by whom he is put into prison. There he displays the power of his divine inspiration, by interpreting aright the dreams of some (fellow-prisoners). Meanwhile the king, too, has some terrible dreams. Joseph being brought before him, according to his summons, was able to expound them. Having narrated the proofs of true interpretation which he had given in the prison, he opens out his dream to the king: those seven fat-fleshed and well-favoured cattle signified as many years of plenty; in like manner, the seven lean-fleshed animals predicted the scarcity of the seven following years. He accordingly recommends precautions to be taken against the future famine from the previous plenty. The king believed him. The issue of all that happened showed how wise he was, how invariably holy, and now how necessary. So Pharaoh set him over all Egypt, that he might secure the provision of grain for it, and thenceforth administer its government. They called him Serapis, from the turban which adorned his head. The peck-like shape of this turban marks the memory of his grain-provisioning; while evidence is given that the care of the supplies was all on his head, by the very ears of grain which embellish the border of the head-dress. For the same reason, also, they made the sacred figure of a dog, which they regard (as a sentry) in Hades, and put it under his right hand, because the care of the Egyptians was concentrated under his hand. And they put at his side Pharia, whose name shows her to have been the king’s daughter. For in addition to all the rest of his kind gifts and rewards, Pharaoh had given him his own daughter in marriage. Since, however, they had begun to worship both wild animals and human beings, they combined both figures under one form Anubis, in which there may rather be seen clear proofs of its own character and condition enshrined by a nation at war with itself, refractory to its kings, despised among foreigners, with even the appetite of a slave and the filthy nature of a dog.

Minucius Felix (2nd–3rd century AD)

The work Octavius is a dialogue in which the Christian speaker Octavius answers the pagan Caecilius point by point. It was composed by the Christian lawyer and apologist Minucius Felix. Chapters 21-23 of this work (source link here) gives one of the clearest statements in the entire corpus of the “ancestor-deification” theory of the origin of the gods:

Octavius argues that earlier generations, out of untrained simplicity, worshipped their kings religiously, wishing to keep the faces of the dead before them in images, and that what had begun merely as a consolation for grief later hardened into sacred cult.

The gods worshipped by the Greeks and Romans were originally ordinary human beings whose stories became exaggerated over time until they were treated as divine. The author cites some fringe ancient pagan historians like Euhemerus who argued that famous gods such as Jupiter (Zeus), Apollo, Isis, Osiris, Ceres, and others were once real people. Their birthdays, tombs, and places of origin were even known, which the author claims proves they were mortal rather than eternal gods. He criticises religious ceremonies and mystery cults and describes rituals surrounding Isis, Osiris, Ceres, and Jupiter as mere dramatic reenactments of death, grief, and suffering. The author argues it is irrational to worship gods who are portrayed as dying, being lost, or needing rescue (Does this sound so modern to you. !!?). The myths themselves are presented as evidence against the gods’ divinity. Many gods are described as flawed, immoral, or physically imperfect: Vulcan is lame, Saturn is chained, Janus has two faces, Pan has goat’s feet, and Jupiter appears in many contradictory forms. The author also blames the ancient poets—especially Homer—for spreading these myths and corrupting people’s beliefs. By portraying the gods behaving immorally, the poets, according to the author, encouraged similar behaviour in ordinary people. Saturn and Jupiter were ultimately fallible historical human rulers rather than divine beings. According to him, the ancient human being Saturn is said to have fled Crete, settled in Italy, introduced useful skills, and given his name to places such as Saturnia, eventually becoming a god. Jupiter likewise is said to have lived, ruled, died, and even had a known tomb, thus demonstrating his humanity.

Finally, having shown that the pagan gods are originally humans, the author rejects the idea that humans can become gods after death. Since all humans are born and eventually die, they cannot be truly divine. True divinity, the author argues, must be eternal—without birth or death. He then concludes as usual by attacking the worship of statues. It points out that idols are simply made from wood, stone, silver, or bronze by human craftsmen. These materials are cut, shaped, assembled, and only become “gods” after people decide to dedicate and worship them. Therefore, the author argues, idols have no inherent divinity and are merely human creations mistaken for gods by the ignorant.

He thus establishes again that although the heathens acknowledge their kings to have been mortal, they nevertheless feign them to be gods, not from any real belief in their divinity but in honour of the power those kings had once wielded, adding pointedly that the true God, unlike these gods, has neither rising nor setting. Minucius Felix even applies the theory to Rome’s founding legend: He recalls that Romulus, the founder of Rome himself, was a human king and then was made a god (Roman tradition itself acknowledges this), which according to the author happened by the false oath of Proculus (a baseless polemical claim). The full extract if you are interested is below.

Read the writings of the Stoics, or the writings of wise men, you will acknowledge these facts with me. On account of the merits of their virtue or of some gift, Euhemerus asserts that they were esteemed gods; and he enumerates their birthdays, their countries, their places of sepulture, and throughout various provinces points out these circumstances of the Dictæan Jupiter, and of the Delphic Apollo, and of the Pharian Isis, and of the Eleusinian Ceres. Prodicus speaks of men who were taken up among the gods, because they were helpful to the uses of men in their wanderings, by the discovery of new kinds of produce. Persæus philosophizes also to the same result; and he adds thereto, that the fruits discovered, and the discoverers of those same fruits, were called by the same names; as the passage of the comic writer runs, that Venus freezes without Bacchus and Ceres. Alexander the Great, the celebrated Macedonian, wrote in a remarkable document addressed to his mother, that under fear of his power there had been betrayed to him by the priest the secret of the gods having been men: to her he makes Vulcan the original of all, and then the race of Jupiter. And you behold the swallow and the cymbal of Isis, and the tomb of your Serapis or Osiris empty, with his limbs scattered about. Then consider the sacred rites themselves, and their very mysteries: you will find mournful deaths, misfortunes, and funerals, and the griefs and wailings of the miserable gods. Isis bewails, laments, and seeks after her lost son, with her Cynocephalus and her bald priests; and the wretched Isiacs beat their breasts, and imitate the grief of the most unhappy mother. By and by, when the little boy is found, Isis rejoices, and the priests exult, Cynocephalus the discoverer boasts, and they do not cease year by year either to lose what they find, or to find what they lose. Is it not ridiculous either to grieve for what you worship, or to worship that over which you grieve? Yet these were formerly Egyptian rites, and now are Roman ones. Ceres with her torches lighted, and surrounded with a serpent, with anxiety and solicitude tracks the footsteps of Proserpine, stolen away in her wandering, and corrupter. These are the Eleusinian mysteries. And what are the sacred rites of Jupiter? His nurse is a she-goat, and as an infant he is taken away from his greedy father, lest he should be devoured; and clanging uproar is dashed out of the cymbals of the Corybantes, lest the father should hear the infant’s wailing. Cybele of Dindymus — I am ashamed to speak of it — who could not entice her adulterous lover, who unhappily was pleasing to her, to lewdness, because she herself, as being the mother of many gods, was ugly and old, mutilated him, doubtless that she might make a god of the eunuch. On account of this story, the Galli also worship her by the punishment of their emasculated body. Now certainly these things are not sacred rites, but tortures. What are the very forms and appearances (of the gods)? Do they not argue the contemptible and disgraceful characters of your gods? Vulcan is a lame god, and crippled; Apollo, smooth-faced after so many ages; Æsculapius well bearded, notwithstanding that he is the son of the ever youthful Apollo; Neptune with sea-green eyes; Minerva with eyes bluish grey; Juno with ox-eyes; Mercury with winged feet; Pan with hoofed feet; Saturn with feet in fetters; Janus, indeed, wears two faces, as if that he might walk with looks turned back; Diana sometimes is a huntress, with her robe girded up high; and as the Ephesian she has many and fruitful breasts; and when exaggerated as Trivia, she is horrible with three heads and with many hands. What is your Jupiter himself? Now he is represented in a statue as beardless, now he is set up as bearded; and when he is called Hammon, he has horns; and when Capitolinus, then he wields the thunderbolts; and when Latiaris, he is sprinkled with gore; and when Feretrius, he is not approached; and not to mention any further the multitude of Jupiters, the monstrous appearances of Jupiter are as numerous as his names. Erigone was hanged from a noose, that as a virgin she might be glowing among the stars. The Castors die by turns, that they may live. Æsculapius, that he may rise into a god, is struck with a thunderbolt. Hercules, that he may put off humanity, is burnt up by the fires of Œta.

These fables and errors we both learn from ignorant parents, and, what is more serious still, we elaborate them in our very studies and instructions, especially in the verses of the poets, who as much as possible have prejudiced the truth by their authority. And for this reason Plato rightly expelled from the state which he had founded in his discourse, the illustrious Homer whom he had praised and crowned. For it was he especially who in the Trojan was allowed your gods, although he made jests of them, still to interfere in the affairs and doings of men: he brought them together in contest; he wounded Venus; he bound, wounded, and drove away Mars. He relates that Jupiter was set free by Briareus, so as not to be bound fast by the rest of the gods; and that he bewailed in showers of blood his son Sarpedon, because he could not snatch him from death; and that, enticed by the girdle of Venus, he lay more eagerly with his wife Juno than he was accustomed to do with his adulterous loves. Elsewhere Hercules threw out dung, and Apollo is feeding cattle for Admetus. Neptune, however, builds walls for Laomedon, and the unfortunate builder did not receive the wages for his work. Then Jupiter’s thunderbolt is fabricated on the anvil with the arms of Æneas, although there were heaven, and thunderbolts, and lightnings long before Jupiter was born in Crete; and neither could the Cyclops imitate, nor Jupiter himself help fearing, the flames of the real thunderbolt. Why should I speak of the detected adultery of Mars and Venus, and of the violence of Jupiter against Ganymede — a deed consecrated, (as you say,) in heaven? And all these things have been put forward with this view, that a certain authority might be gained for the vices of men. By these fictions, and such as these, and by lies of a more attractive kind, the minds of boys are corrupted; and with the same fables clinging to them, they grow up even to the strength of mature age; and, poor wretches, they grow old in the same beliefs, although the truth is plain, if they will only seek after it. For all the writers of antiquity, both Greek and Roman, have set forth that Saturn, the beginner of this race and multitude, was a man. Nepos knows this, and Cassius in his history; and Thallus and Diodorus speak the same thing. This Saturn then, driven from Crete, by the fear of his raging son, had come to Italy, and, received by the hospitality of Janus, taught those unskilled and rustic men many things — as, being something of a Greek, and polished — to print letters for instance, to coin money, to make instruments. Therefore he preferred that his hiding-place, because he had been safely hidden (latent) there, should be called Latium; and he gave a city, from his own name, the name of Saturnia, and Janus, Janiculum, so that each of them left their names to the memory of posterity. Therefore it was certainly a man that fled, certainly a man who was concealed, and the father of a man, and sprung from a man. He was declared, however, to be the son of earth or of heaven, because among the Italians he was of unknown parents; as even to this day we call those who appear unexpectedly, sent from heaven, those who are ignoble and unknown, sons of the earth. His son Jupiter reigned at Crete after his father was driven out. There he died, there he had sons. To this day the cave of Jupiter is visited, and his sepulchre is shown, and he is convicted of being human by those very sacred rites of his.

It is needless to go through each individual case, and to develop the entire series of that race, since in its first parents their mortality is proved, and must have flowed down into the rest by the very law of their succession, unless perhaps you fancy that they were gods after death; as by the perjury of Proculus, Romulus became a god; and by the good-will of the Mauritanians, Juba is a god; and other kings are divine who are consecrated, not in the faith of their divinity, but in honour of the power that they exercised. Moreover, this name is ascribed to those who are unwilling to bear it. They desire to persevere in their human condition. They fear that they may be made gods; although they are already old men, they do not wish it. Therefore neither are gods made from dead people, since a god cannot die; nor of people that are born, since everything which is born dies. But that is divine which has neither rising nor setting. For why, if they were born, are they not born in the present day also?— unless, perchance, Jupiter has already grown old, and child-bearing has failed in Juno, and Minerva has grown grey before she has borne children. Or has that process of generation ceased, for the reason that no assent is any longer yielded to fables of this kind? Besides, if the gods could create, they could not perish: we should have more gods than all men together; so that now, neither would the heaven contain them, nor the air receive them, nor the earth bear them. Whence it is manifest, that those were men whom we both read of as having been born, and know to have died. Who therefore doubts that the common people pray to and publicly worship the consecrated images of these men; in that the belief and mind of the ignorant is deceived by the perfection of art, is blinded by the glitter of gold, is dimmed with the shining of silver and the whiteness of ivory? But if any one were to present to his mind with what instruments and with what machinery every image is formed, he would blush that he had feared matter, treated after his fancy by the artificer to make a god. For a god of wood, a portion perhaps of a pile, or of an unlucky log, is hung up, is cut, is hewn, is planed; and a god of brass or of silver, often from an impure vessel, as was done by the Egyptian king, is fused, is beaten with hammers and forged on anvils; and the god of stone is cut, is sculptured, and is polished by some abandoned man, nor feels the injury done to him in his nativity, any more than afterwards it feels the worship flowing from your veneration; unless perhaps the stone, or the wood, or the silver is not yet a god. When, therefore, does the god begin his existence? Lo, it is melted, it is wrought, it is sculptured — it is not yet a god; lo, it is soldered, it is built together — it is set up, and even yet it is not a god; lo, it is adorned, it is consecrated, it is prayed to — then at length it is a god, when man has chosen it to be so, and for the purpose has dedicated it.

Cyprian of Carthage (mid-3rd century AD)

The church father Cyprian’s short treatise Quod Idola Dii Non Sint ("On the Vanity of Idols") states the euhemerist thesis as its literal organizing principle. He opens the argument bluntly (source link here) by explaining that these figures were formerly kings who, on account of the people’s memory of their royal rank, began to be adored even after death ; hence had temples founded for them, the statues carved to preserve the dead man’s likeness, and the sacrifices and festival days offered by way of honour, so that what had first been adopted merely as consolation later became, for posterity, sacred ritual.

He tells that since all pagan gods are deified ancestors, this is the reason why pagan gods are ethnic and local (each nation has its own set of deified ancestors) unlike the one true God of the Christians who was not sectarian and ought to be worshipped as the only One who created the entire universe.

He runs through a rapid tour of comparative religion, treating Melicertes, the Dioscuri, Aesculapius, Hercules, Saturn, and Picus all as historical men subsequently divinized, and adds the memorable detail on Rome’s own civic cult where Romulus was made a god through the perjury of Proculus, and that Picus, Tiberinus, Pilumnus and Consus were likewise elevated, along with the invented deities Cloacina, Fear, Paleness, Fever, and the deified courtesans Acca and Flora. An original extract from his work is given below.

That those are no gods whom the common people worship, is known from this. They were formerly kings, who on account of their royal memory subsequently began to be adored by their people even in death. Thence temples were founded to them; thence images were sculptured to retain the countenances of the deceased by the likeness; and men sacrificed victims, and celebrated festal days, by way of giving them honour. Thence to posterity those rites became sacred which at first had been adopted as a consolation. And now let us see whether this truth is confirmed in individual instances.

Melicertes and Leucothea are precipitated into the sea, and subsequently become sea-divinities. The Castors die by turns, that they may live. Aesculapius is struck by lightning, that he may rise into a god. Hercules, that he may put off the man, is burnt up in the fires of Oeta. Apollo fed the flocks of Admetus; Neptune founded walls for Laomedon, and received — unfortunate builder — no wages for his work. The cave of Jupiter is to be seen in Crete, and his sepulchre is shown; and it is manifest that Saturn was driven away by him, and that from him Latium received its name, as being his lurking-place. He was the first that taught to print letters; he was the first that taught to stamp money in Italy, and thence the treasury is called the treasury of Saturn. And he also was the cultivator of the rustic life, whence he is painted as an old man carrying a sickle. Janus had received him to hospitality when he was driven away, from whose name the Janiculum is so called, and the month of January is appointed. He himself is portrayed with two faces, because, placed in the middle, he seems to look equally towards the commencing and the closing year. The Mauri, indeed, manifestly worship kings, and do not conceal their name by any disguise.

From this the religion of the gods is variously changed among individual nations and provinces, inasmuch as no one god is worshipped by all, but by each one the worship of its own ancestors is kept peculiar. Proving that this is so, Alexander the Great writes in the remarkable volume addressed to his mother, that through fear of his power, the doctrine of the gods being men, which was kept secret, had been disclosed to him by a priest, that it was the memory of ancestors and kings that was (really) kept up, and that from this the rites of worship and sacrifice have grown up. But if gods were born at any time, why are they not born in these days also?— unless, indeed, Jupiter possibly has grown too old, or the faculty of bearing has failed Juno.

Lactantius (early 4th century AD)

Lactantius is the single most important conduit for Euhemerus’s own text into later ages, because he quotes Ennius’s Latin translation of Euhemerus directly and at length in Book I of his work titled Divine Institutes. He introduces his source with care where in he describes Euhemerus as an ancient author of the city of Messene who collected the deeds of Jupiter and the other so-called gods, composing a history drawn from the titles and sacred inscriptions found in the most ancient temples, above all in the shrine of the Triphylian Jupiter, where an inscription recorded that Jupiter himself had set up a golden column on which he wrote an account of his own exploits for posterity.

Lactantius then extracts the genealogy given in this “sacred history” of the gods. According to him, Euhemerus records that Uranus was the first to reign on earth, having established that kingdom for himself together with his brothers, and that Jupiter, after circling the world five times, distributing kingdoms to his friends and kinsmen, and giving men their laws, at last died in Crete — his tomb still shown at Gnossus, inscribed in ancient Greek letters meaning “Jupiter, son of Saturn. Lactantius draws the explicit theological conclusion in his chapter headings, one of which asks simply “how they who were men obtained the name of gods.“He extends the same method to purely Roman cults. Quoting from Chapter 13 of the work, we find (source link here) :

Chapter 13

But let us leave the poets; let us come to history, which is supported both by the credibility of the facts and by the antiquity of the times. Euhemerus was a Messenian, a very ancient writer, who gave an account of the origin of Jupiter, and his exploits, and all his posterity, gathered from the sacred inscriptions of ancient temples; he also traced out the parents of the other gods, their countries, actions, commands, and deaths, and even their sepulchres (graves). And this history Ennius translated into Latin, whose words are these:—

As these things are written, so is the origin and kindred of Jupiter and his brothers, after this manner it is handed clown to us in the sacred writing.

The same Euhemerus therefore relates that Jupiter, when he had five times gone round the world, and had distributed governments to his friends and relatives, and had given laws to men, and had wrought many other benefits, being endued with immortal glory and everlasting remembrance, ended his life in Crete, and departed to the gods, and that his sepulchre is in Crete, in the town of Gnossus, and that upon it is engraved in ancient Greek letters Zankronou, which is Jupiter, the son of Saturnus. It is plain, therefore, from the things which I have related, that he (Jupiter) was a man, and reigned on the earth.

Chapter 24

… Euhemerus, in his sacred history, says that Jupiter himself, after that he received the government, erected temples in honour of himself in many places. For in going about the world, as he came to each place he united the chiefs of the people to himself in friendship and the right of hospitality; and that the remembrance of this might be preserved, he ordered that temples should be built to him, and annual festivals be celebrated by those connected with him in a league of hospitality. Thus he spread the worship of himself through all lands. But at what time they lived can easily be inferred. For Thallus writes in his history, that Belus, the king of the Assyrians, whom the Babylonians worship, and who was the contemporary and friend of Saturnus, was three hundred and twenty-two years before the Trojan War, and it is fourteen hundred and seventy years since the taking of Troy. From which it is evident, that it is not more than eighteen hundred years from the time when mankind fell into error by the institution of new forms of divine worship.

Firmicus Maternus (c. 346–350 AD)

Writing after Constantine, when Christianity was the state-favored religion, the converted astrologer Julius Firmicus Maternus in his work De Errore Profanarum Religionum (“On the Error of the Pagan Religions”) addresssed to the emperors Constans and Constantius, here calls explicitly for state suppression of paganism. His method in the first half of the treatise follows the same track as his predecessors. He ridicules the pagan gods by dwelling on their crimes and immorality and by arguing that the pagan gods were nothing more than personified natural elements or processes or dead deified ancestors — arguments that were basically inherited from earlier Christian apologists. Where Firmicus differs from the second-century apologists is in tone rather than substance: he treats the mortal, historical origin of gods like Osiris, Isis, and the Cretan Jupiter as already so well established (source link here) by his predecessors that he can move quickly to demanding the destruction of their temples.

St Augustine of Hippo (Early 5th century AD)

St Augustine’s work The City of God is where patristic euhemerism reaches its most systematic and historically ambitious form, because Augustine is trying to build an entire synchronised world chronology (pagan kings against biblical judges and prophets) in Books VI–XVIII. In Book VI, discussing the contradictions in Roman theology, he invokes Euhemerus by name as a serious historian rather than a mere fabulist or a theorist. Augustine asks whether the placing of Jupiter’s wet-nurse in the Capitol did not itself bear witness to the claims of Euhemerus that he was a human (only humans needed caring by wet-nurses) and that all such gods had been men and mortals. In Book XVIII, Augustine works through the Italian king-list, treating Saturn, Picus, and Faunus as literal, datable human kings whose subsequent divinisation he narrates as a straightforward historical event. He explains that the Laurentine kingdom’s founders, Sterces (or Stercutius) and his son Picus — the former discovered that dung fertilized fields, the latter a famous augur and warrior — were both made gods for their merit, and that this happened, notably, “before the Trojan war. He drives the point home for Saturn specifically./ Augustine remarks that the worshippers of such gods should consider what they are to think of Saturn, Picus’s father, whom some deny to have been a man at all, even though other writers record that he himself once reigned in Italy before his son Picus did. He writes in Book VI, Chapter 7 (source link here) :

What was thought of Jupiter himself by those who placed his wet nurse in the Capitol? Did they not bear witness to Euhemerus, who, not with the garrulity of a fable-teller, but with the gravity of an historian who had diligently investigated the matter, wrote that all such gods had been men and mortals? And they who appointed the Epulones as parasites at the table of Jupiter, what else did they wish for but mimic sacred rites. For if any mimic had said that parasites of Jupiter were made use of at his table, he would assuredly have appeared to be seeking to call forth laughter. Varro said it — not when he was mocking, but when he was commending the gods did he say it. His books on divine, not on human, things testify that he wrote this — not where he set forth the scenic games, but where he explained the Capitoline laws. In a word, he is conquered, and confesses that, as they made the gods with a human form, so they believed that they are delighted with human pleasures.

Eusebius of Caesarea (early 4th century AD)

A brief note belongs here even without a directly quoted passage recovered for this article. The Roman church father Eusebius’s work Praeparatio Evangelica (Preparation for the Gospel) preserves, via Philo of Byblos’s Greek rendering of the (probably invented) Phoenician priest Sanchuniathon, an elaborate euhemerist genealogy of the major Phoenician gods like El/Cronus, Baal, Astarte and others - where in these gods are portrayed as early kings of Byblos and Tyre. This is a material Eusebius reproduces at length precisely (it can be read here) because it served his larger argument, spanning the whole of the Praeparatio, that pagan theology reduces on inspection to garbled human history.

The Medieval Period : Euhemerism for Cultural Preservation

By the time Christianity had converted most of the Roman empire and was slowly gaining inroads in the rest of Europe, euhemerism’s job description had subtly changed. The early apologists used it offensively, against a living, competing religion, to downplay it. Medieval Christian writers in newly converted regions faced a huge dilemma because vernacular heroic poetry about the old gods of their pagan religions was too culturally valuable to simply get rid of. Hence, they employed the same theory defensively to justify preserving pagan material as history and literature rather than religion.

Brigid of Ireland (c. 600 AD)

This case is novel because it works by a slightly different mechanism than the classic patristic “your god was a man” argument. Tertullian or Cyprian argue that a named god was originally a man; nobody at Kildare in Ireland ever wrote a treatise arguing that the Irish pagan goddess Brigid was secretly the historical Christian nun Brigid of the same name. What happened instead, over some five centuries of Irish hagiography, was a cumulative absorption: an early probably historical Christian abbess (probably historical, d. c. 524) inherited the name, the feast day, the cult-sites, the fire-cult, and the professional patronages of a pre-Christian goddess of the same name. One recent academic study is titled simply “Brigit: Goddess, Saint, ‘Holy Woman**,’ and Bone of Contention**,” and another, by the historian Lisa Bitel, calls its subject “St. Brigit of Ireland: From Virgin Saint to Fertility Goddess.” A blog survey of the primary evidence sums up the whole trajectory in its own title: “From Cogitosus to Giraldus Cambrensis — Brigit replaces the Goddess.”

The goddess : The evidence for a pre-Christian goddess Brigid rests mainly on the 9th–10th-century Sanas Cormaic (”Cormac’s Glossary”), compiled by Cormac mac Cuilennáin, bishop-king of Cashel — a Christian scholarly source, notably, cataloguing a pagan deity that it treats as safely defunct. The glossary’s entry reads: “Brigit, i.e., a poetess, daughter of the Dagda… the goddess whom poets adored, because very great and very famous was her protecting care.” Cormac adds that she had two sisters of the same name, patronesses of healing and smithcraft respectively, “from whose names all Irishmen [called] a goddess Brigit” implying a triple deity of poetry, healing and craft whose name became a generic word for “goddess” in Irish.

The saint absorbs the goddess’s calendar and cult-site : Cogitosus, a monk of Kildare writing his Vita Sanctae Brigidae around 650 AD, the oldest surviving life of the saint the first to mention a feast day of Saint Brigid being observed in Kildare on 1 February, which was also the date of Imbolc, the pre-Christian Irish festival marking the start of spring lambing. Cogitosus frames his subject in fully orthodox Christian terms from his opening line: “The holy Brigid, whom God knew beforehand and whom he predestined to be moulded in his image.” Yet the saint he describes performs functions like multiplying butter and milk, blessing livestock, presiding over an important fire all of which read as a checklist of the goddess Brigid’s own attributes transposed onto a nun.

The perpetual fire : The clearest single piece of continuity is the sacred fire kept at Kildare, tended (per later, elaborated tradition) by nineteen nuns in rotation, with Brigid herself said to keep the twentieth watch, and surrounded by a hedge that no man was permitted to cross. Writing five centuries after Cogitosus, the Cambro-Norman churchman Gerald of Wales (Giraldus Cambrensis) visited Kildare around 1185 and described the flame still burning under his own eyes: “this fire… is called inextinguishable, not that it could not be extinguished, but because the nuns feed it with fuel so carefully that it has ever continued inextinct from the time of the virgin,” adding that in Brigid’s own lifetime “twenty nuns served their Lord there, she making the twentieth,” and that only nineteen have kept the watch since her death. Brigid’s own place, it seems, was being considered permanently unfillable. A perpetual sacred fire tended exclusively by women, on a hilltop later re-dedicated to a female saint who shares the goddess’s name, patronages, and calendar date, is about as strong a case of ritual continuity as survives anywhere, even though, again, no medieval Christian author states outright “the goddess Brigid was merely a woman ; a Christian nun who lived fairly recently”: the demotion happens by gradual substitution and calendar capture rather than by argument.

Isidore of Seville (c. 615–630 AD)

Isidore’s celebrated work Etymologiae (source link here), the single most copied and read reference work of the entire Latin Middle Ages. It is the equivalent of an Encyclopedia Brtiannica for the medieval world. In Book VIII on religion, he includes a section on “the gods of the gentiles” (De diis gentium). Isidore’s approach there which involves treating the pagan pantheon under the discipline of etymology and euhemerist history, rather than live theological threat, became the standard medieval schoolroom account of who the pagan gods “really” were; the topic was influential enough to be the subject of a dedicated modern scholarly study. Working two centuries after Augustine, Isidore treats the Euhemerian tradition filtered through early Christianity as settled background knowledge rather than something needing fresh argument which is itself a sign of how completely the euhemerist explanation had become the default Christian account of where the old gods came from. In his section, he writes :

Those who the pagans assert are gods are revealed to have once been humans, and after their death they began to be worshipped among their people because of the life and merit of each of them, as Isis in Egypt, Jupiter in Crete, Iuba among the Moors, Faunus among the Latins, and Quirinus among the Romans. It was the same with Minerva in Athens, Juno in Samos, Venus in Paphos, Vulcan in Lemnos, Liber in Naxos and Apollo in Delos. Poets joined in their praises of these, and by the songs they composed carried them up to the sky. In the case of some of them people are said to have brought about the discovery of arts through their cults: there is medicine for Aesculapius, forging for Vulcan. Further, they are named from their activities, as Mercury (Mercurius), because he excels at commerce (merx), Liber (Liber) from liberty (libertas). Again, there were certain powerful men, or founders of cities, for whom, after they had died, the people who had been fond of them made likenesses, so that they might have some solace from contemplating these images. However, at the urging of demons, this error gradually crept into later generations in such a way that those, whom people had honoured only for the memory of their name, their successors deemed as gods and worshipped.

The use of likenesses arose when, out of grief for the dead, images or effigies were set up, as if in place of those who had been received into heaven, demons substituted themselves to be worshipped on earth, and persuaded deceived and lost people to make sacrifices to themselves. And ‘likenesses’ (simulacrum) are named from ‘similarity’ (similitudo), because, through the hand of an artisan, the faces of those in whose honour the likenesses are constructed are imitated in stone or some other material. Therefore they are called likenesses either because they are similar (similis), or because they are feigned (simulare) or invented, whence they are false.

Beowulf (c. 700-1000)

The Anglo-Saxon epic poem Beowulf is the most popular work in Old English. Beowulf gives two distinct, and distinctly interesting, examples of the same Christian reflex, one aimed at gods and one aimed at monsters.

Scyld Scefing and the god Ingvi-Freyr : The poem opens not with Beowulf but with the ancestor-list of the Danish royal line, starting from the mysterious founding king Scyld Scefing, who arrives from the sea as a destitute child and is given a ship-burial when he dies. Modern Beowulf scholarship has long suspected that this founder-figure, and the related name Ing invoked elsewhere in Old English tradition (including the Old English Rune Poem), are Christianised survivals of the Germanic fertility god Ingvi-Freyr, worshipped with real cult at Uppsala according to both Adam of Bremen and Saxo Grammaticus. One recent study (source link here) puts the pattern in exactly euhemerist terms: “Old Norse prose texts of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries are thought to euhemerize this figure as a human king”.

The poem’s own account of Scyld is worth quoting directly for comparison — it describes him only as a great and mysterious human king, never a god: “That was a good king!“ the poet says flatly of Scyld’s rise from destitution to dominance, immediately followed by the notice that his son Beow’s fame “sprang widely… throughout all the northern lands” — precisely the kind of studied, deliberate silence about divinity that a euhemerizing Christian poet would produce if working with inherited god-legend.

Grendel and the “kin of Cain.” Where the Scyld material euhemerizes a god into a man, the poem performs a related but distinct maneuver on the monsters: rather than leaving Grendel and his mother as unexplained native supernatural beings, the poet anchors them in biblical genealogy, deriving all of Anglo-Saxon monster-lore from the biblical murderer Cain. The poet writes that “Of Cain awoke all that woful breed, / Etins and elves and evil-spirits, / as well as the giants that warred with God” — folding the indigenous Germanic taxonomy of monsters (eotenas, elves, orcneas) directly into the family tree of Genesis, so that native folklore becomes an appendix to biblical history rather than a rival mythology. This is not quite the “your god was a man” move of Athenagoras or Ælfric, but it is the same underlying instinct applied one category over: pre-Christian supernatural beings, whether gods or monsters, get absorbed into a single Christian historical framework rather than left as independently existing supernatural beings.

Snorri Sturluson and the Prose Edda (Iceland, c. 1220)

The most famous and most deliberately constructed medieval instance of Christian euhemerism applied to a whole pagan pantheon is the prologue of the work of the Icelandic chieftain and Christian scholar Snorri Sturluson. His text The Prose Edda, along with his The Poetic Edda, is the only text that preserves almost everything we know about Norse mythology of the Vikings whose descendants constitute the modern Scandinavian nations. Snorri was a Christian and when he was writing, Iceland (the last Scandinavian nation to convert to Christianity) was already Christian but its pagan myths were still being in living memory amongst the Icelandics. Snorri intended to write his Eddas to preserve this pagan mythic inheritance of his nation before it was lost for posterity under the sweep of Christianity. In the Prose Edda (source link here) , he opens not with the Norse gods Odin and Thor but with Genesis from the Bible. He says : “In the beginning almighty God created heaven and earth and everything that goes with them and, last of all, two human beings, Adam and Eve, from whom have come families”. He then works forward through a euhemerist genealogy that identifies the Æsir — the Norse gods — as a migrating dynasty of impressive but entirely mortal men from Asia Minor. Snorri traces the lineage of the Æsir back to the royal line of Troy, making the Norse god Thor, the mortal grandson of the Trojan king Priam and the god Odin as his descendant; because Odin and his people from Asia Minor were technologically far more advanced than the northern peoples they encountered in Scandinavia, Snori claims that they came to be regarded as gods. Snori also says that “Æsir” (the Norse word for gods) is simply the Norse word for “men of Asia”. According to him, King Gylfi welcomed Odin and his retinue as “men of Asia, who were called Æsir”; in Sweden, Odin founded the city of Sigtúna as a Trojan colony, his son Yngvi became king of Sweden and founded the Yngling dynasty, and Odin finally went on to Norway, where he installed his son Sæmingr as king. This clearly exposes his underlying strategic logic in exactly euhemerist terms. Medieval Christian intellectuals found this framework useful because it let them engage with pagan material without endorsing it while still enjoying it and preserving it because the pagan gods were not real divinities (as the Church would want it) but memorable human beings incorrectly deified by later generations who did not know better.

Saxo Grammaticus and the Gesta Danorum (Denmark, c. 1200–1216)

Saxo Grammaticus, a cleric writing for the Archbishop of Lund, applied a harsher version of the same technique to the Danish material in his work Gesta Danorum. Whereas Snorri’s work euhemerized gods are essentially benign migrant kings, Saxo’s Odin and the other Æsir are depicted as outright confidence tricksters; human sorcerers who used illusion and stagecraft to extort worship from the credulous. A recent scholarly treatment describes his method precisely. Saxo, the first Scandinavian author to produce extended narratives about the pagan gods, used euhemerism in a stronger form than Snorri. For Saxo, the “gods” were sorcerers, gifted illusionists rather than ordinary men, who deceived the pagans of ancient times. He portrays Odin as a pure deceiver, in this way exposing the foolish superstition of the pagans and the worship of creatures who were in reality nothing more than ordinary people. Encyclopedia Britannica summarizes the entire nine-book “mythical” section of the Gesta Danorum in the same terms as (link here) :

The first nine books of this work deal with the prehistory of the Danes and are actually a history of the ancient gods and heroes. Interpreting the old religion euhemeristically (i.e., by reducing the gods to the level of distinguished men), Saxo regarded the pagan gods chiefly as crafty men of old.

Saxo’s motive, per the same modern account, was topical rather than purely antiquarian because pre-Christian religion had not yet fully disappeared in Denmark in Saxo’s own day, it mattered to him not to speak of the old gods with any respect, given the real risk of pagan relapse among his contemporaries.

Crossing the Ocean: Euhemerism Applied to the Americas

When Spanish (and later English) missionaries and chroniclers encountered the religious systems of Mesoamerica and the Andes, they reached immediately for the same interpretive toolkit that Christian writers had used on gods like Jupiter and Odin. The pattern took two related forms in the New World:

(a) treating gods (Quetzalcoatl above all, and the Andean creator-figure Viracocha) as historical men sometimes even identified with Christian missionary saints who had supposedly reached America in antiquity;

(b) treating the whole apparatus of Inca and Aztec “state religion” as the deification of real kings and queens, on the explicit model of Roman apotheosis.

José de Acosta (1590)

Jose de Acosta, a Spanish Jesuit who spent years in Peru and Mexico, wrote what became the single most influential and widely translated account of Amerindian religion for a European audience. His work Historia Natural y Moral de las Indias (Moral History) in Books V–VII, is organized around the working assumption that most Andean and Mexican “idolatry” is explicable through recognisable human categories: nature-worship, ancestor- and huaca- (spirit) worship, and the straightforward deification of dead rulers and heroic founders, a category he treats as needing no more explanation than Roman apotheosis did for Augustine. The very organisation of his book signals this: chapters on “idolatry” sit immediately beside chapters explaining how Aztec practice reserved a distinct, (and to Acosta), a bizarre, category of idolatry built around images and statues, a phenomenon he treats at length as a strange variety worth a chapter of its own. Acosta’s Historia is remembered as an effort to incorporate new information about the Americas into a Christian, Renaissance framework, that is, to make Andean and Mexican religious history, legible in the same terms already worked out for Greek and Roman and other European religious history, over the preceding twelve centuries.

Fray Diego Durán (finished c. 1579) : St Thomas in Mexico

Fray Diego Durán, a Dominican friar raised in Mexico and fluent in the language of Nahuatl, produced the fullest Spanish account Book of the Gods and Rites and the Ancient Calendar of the Aztec pantheon and calendar available in the sixteenth century. His treatment of the Aztec creator god Quetzalcoatl (whose human incarnate priestly name Topiltzin) is the New World’s most vivid single case of “your god was a man,” with an additional twist: Durán does not merely demote Topiltzin-Quetzalcoatl to a mortal, he promotes him into a Christian missionary, identifying him with the apostle Saint Thomas, traditionally believed to have evangelised India !!

The legend is as follows. Quetzalcoatl (who was actually St Thomas) was described as a bearded, white-skinned man (instead of a serpent bodied god) who advocated for peace, knowledge, and virtue. He famously opposed human sacrifice and taught a way of life that friars compared to Christian monasticism. The presence of some apparently Christian-shaped crosses on ancient Mexican monuments (such as those observed by the Spanish at Veracruz) convinced early missionaries that an Apostle had already reached the continent. Like the legends surrounding the Apostles, Quetzalcoatl eventually is said to have left, sailing away on a raft of serpents (which later devolved into his depiction as the snake bodied god) with a promise to return.

A modern digest of Durán’s argument states the sequence exactly:

Durán held that God had sent Saint Thomas — whom the Mexicans knew as Topiltzin-Quetzalcoatl — to evangelise these peoples and turn them back from the idolatry into which they had fallen, until his rival Tetzcatlipoca (whom also the Aztecs worshipped a a god) rose up against him. After the ensuing wars that destroyed Toltec Tula, drove Topiltzin-Quetzalcoatl into exile, from which he prophesied his eventual return.

Durán’s own words, as rendered in a widely circulated modern extract, make the euhemerist demotion explicit even as they promote the figure into sanctity : he writes

that venerable man whom we call Topiltzin , …. was, according to indigenous traditions, a chaste and penitent priest who had periods of miracles… we can probably consider him a holy man, an apostle that God sent to earth.

The theological logic here is worth pausing on: the god is stripped of divinity (demoted to “simply a man,” “a priest” by reducing him to just his human incarnation), in the very same sentence that reclassifies him under a different, safely subordinate Christian species of the sacred (saint, apostle) which is a distinctively Christian variant on Euhemerus’s original move, in which the deified man is not merely a historical curiosity but a piece of Christian salvation history.

Inca Garcilaso de la Vega (c. 1609)

Inca Garcilaso de la Vaga, the mestizo son of a Spanish conquistador and an Inca princess, wrote from the inside of the tradition he was simultaneously euhemerizing, and his work Comentarios Reales (Royal Commentaries of the Incas) is explicit that the first Inca, Manco Cápac, was a historical political founder who invented sun-worship as a matter of statecraft rather than receiving it as revealed truth. Garcilaso’s own summary states this without any hedging:

He explains that Manco Cápac, having taught the Indians how to live in political society — to sow, to build, to organize a household — also taught them to adore the Sun, presenting his people’s veneration of the Sun as a deliberate teaching imposed by a human ruler for the people’s benefit, rather than as a genuine divine relationship.

Even just reading the chapter headings of Part I of the Royal Commentaries explicitly show Garcilaso applying exactly the demotion-and-correction logic surveyed throughout this article to the entire Inca state religion: chapters are devoted to explaining how the Incas glimpsed the true God even through their idolatry, and how many of the gods later attributed to the Indians by Spanish historians were in fact wrongly attributed altogether. That is, some of what the Spanish took for Andean polytheism was, on Garcilaso’s own showing, either garbled reporting or the residue of defunct royal cult, not living divine belief.

Bernardino de Sahagún and the wider missionary-ethnographic tradition

Bernardino de Sahagún’s work Historia General de las Cosas de Nueva España (the Florentine Codex) is generally regarded as the most systematic and least polemical of the sixteenth-century Franciscan ethnographies of the Aztec world, but even Sahagún’s project was explicitly framed as a tool for uprooting idolatry by first understanding it as a specific, catalogue-able set of human customs and historical figures. It employs the same underlying premise that the “gods” are functionally explicable historical-cultural phenomena rather than living supernatural beings. This is that drives the whole tradition surveyed in this article, applied by a missionary who wanted his fellow friars to be able to recognise surviving pagan practice in order to suppress it. The wider missionary literature of New Spain and Peru like visitas de idolatría records, catechetical instructions from the Third Council of Lima (1583), Motolinía’s Historia de los Indios de la Nueva España, all of them repeatedly frames Andean huacas and Mexican teotl figures using the same vocabulary of dead rulers, ancestors, and invented benefactors later worshipped by their descendants, precisely mirroring Cyprian’s and Minucius Felix’s treatment of Romulus, Aesculapius, and the rest thirteen centuries earlier.

Conclusion: One Argument, Two Millennia, Many Continents

From Athenagoras writing to Marcus Aurelius in the 170s to Garcilaso writing about his own Inca ancestors in 1609,

Christian writers confronting a rival religious system reached again and again for the same move: concede that the god existed, as a man, and then deny that a man — however great, however useful, however long dead — could possibly be a god.

The argument travelled well because it was flexible in exactly the ways Christian polemicists needed. It could be hostile (Tertullian’s “on what grounds did they become gods?”, Saxo’s sorcerer-Odin as deliberate fraud) or genuinely admiring (Durán’s promotion of Topiltzin-Quetzalcoatl into an apostle). It could serve outright religious replacement, as with the second-century apologists writing under active or threatened persecution, or cultural preservation, as with Snorri safeguarding Norse poetry two centuries after Iceland’s conversion. And it could be deployed by a total outsider to the culture in question (Firmicus Maternus on Egyptian and Levantine cult) or by a writer with deep personal roots inside it (Garcilaso on his own grandfather’s religion).

What stays constant across all of these settings — Rome, Carthage, Iceland, Denmark, Mexico, Peru — was the underlying logical shape first given currency by a Greek writer on a fictional island in the Indian Ocean around 300 BC: the gods of the enemy’s religion are not gods at all, but men; and once they are men, their temples, festivals, and priesthoods become historical curiosities that a Christian scholar can study, catalogue, quote approvingly, and ultimately absorb, rather than a live religious threat that must simply be destroyed.

What is happening to Murugan is just another replay of this strategy.
